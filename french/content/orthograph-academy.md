# The Orthograph Academy — 60+ Spelling Rules

> *"L'orthographe est la science des imbéciles."*
> — Arthur Schopenhauer (quoted approvingly by some French students, and with horror by their teachers)

French spelling is the most complex orthographic system of any major European language — and the most beautiful, once you understand why it is the way it is. The accents are not decorations. The silent letters are not mistakes. The doubled consonants are not redundant. Every rule in this section explains *why*, not just *what*. Once you know why, the rule becomes inevitable rather than arbitrary.

*Note: The sections below mark REFORMED spellings (1990 reforms) where relevant. Both traditional and reformed spellings are accepted in current French education.*

---

## A1 — THE FIVE ACCENTS AND THE CEDILLA

---

### ORTH-01 — The Five Accents

French uses five diacritical marks. This is not cosmetic.

**1. L'accent aigu (é):** Appears only on *e*. Indicates a closed [e] sound — the "ay" sound in English "say." *L'été* (summer), *écouter* (to listen), *bébé* (baby). Origin: marks the sound that resulted from the Latin *es* → *é* (e.g., *schola* → *école*; the *s* disappeared and the *é* records its ghost).

**2. L'accent grave (è, à, ù):**
- On *e*: indicates an open [ɛ] sound — the "e" in English "bed." *Père* (father), *mère* (mother), *règle* (rule).
- On *à*: purely orthographic — distinguishes *à* (preposition: at/to) from *a* (verb: has). No change in pronunciation.
- On *ù*: purely orthographic — distinguishes *où* (where) from *ou* (or). No change in pronunciation.

**3. L'accent circonflexe (â, ê, î, ô, û):**
- Marks a historically long vowel (now rarely pronounced differently).
- Often marks the disappearance of an *s*: *hôpital* ← Latin *hospitale* (hence English "hospital"); *forêt* ← Old French *forest*; *bête* ← *beste* (beast); *fête* ← *feste* (feast).
- **The circumflex is the fossil of a missing *s*.** Knowing this, you can often predict English cognates: *arrêt* (arrest), *île* (isle/island), *côte* (coast), *château* (castle).

**4. L'accent tréma (ë, ï, ü, ÿ):** Indicates that two adjacent vowels are pronounced separately. *Noël* [no.ɛl] — not [nwɛl]. *Naïf* [na.if] — not [nɛf]. *Haïti* [a.i.ti].

**5. La cédille (ç):** Placed under *c* before *a, o, u* to produce the [s] sound instead of [k]. *Garçon* [gar.sɔ̃] (without cédille: *garcon* would be [gar.kɔ̃]). *François*, *leçon*, *façon*. The cédille comes from the Iberian tradition — it is the letter *c* with a small *z* below it (*cedilla* = little *zeta*).

---

### ORTH-02 — Capital Letters and Accents

**The rule:** Accents must be written on capital letters in formal French. *L'État* (not *L'Etat*). *À Paris* (not *A Paris*). *Élève* (not *Eleve*).

This rule is routinely broken in informal writing and on keyboards. Formally, however, *une ÉLÈVE, L'ÉTAT, À L'ÉCOLE* are correct, and omitting accents on capitals in formal documents (diplomas, letters to institutions, official correspondence) is considered an error.

**The cultural note:** The Académie française re-affirmed in 1990 that accents on capitals are obligatory in formal French. The practice of omitting them comes from early typewriter technology, which lacked capital letter accent keys — a mechanical limitation that became a habit. The correct habit is to use them.

---

### ORTH-03 — Elision: When Letters Disappear Before Vowels

**The rule:** Certain one-syllable words ending in a vowel drop that vowel before a word beginning with a vowel or *h aspiré* (see below):

*le + arbre → l'arbre; la + école → l'école; je + aime → j'aime; de + eau → de l'eau; ce + est → c'est; que + il → qu'il; ne + arrive → n'arrive*

**Obligatory elision words:** *le, la, je, me, te, se, de, ne, ce, que* + vowel.

**The *h* issue:** French *h* is either *aspiré* (aspirated — blocking elision) or *muet* (mute — allowing elision).
- *H muet*: *l'homme, l'heure, l'hôpital* — elision occurs.
- *H aspiré*: *le hibou, la honte, le haricot* — NO elision (the *h* acts as a consonant barrier, even though it is not pronounced). *Le hibou*, not *l'hibou*. This must be learned word by word — there is no phonetic way to tell.

---

### ORTH-04 — Liaison: The Traveling Consonant

**The rule:** In certain phonetic environments, the normally silent final consonant of a word is pronounced when the next word begins with a vowel.

*les enfants* → [le.zɑ̃.fɑ̃] — the *s* of *les* travels.
*vous avez* → [vu.za.ve] — the *s* of *vous* travels.
*un homme* → [œ̃.nɔm] — the *n* of *un* travels.
*grand ami* → [gʁɑ̃.ta.mi] — the *d* of *grand* becomes *t* in liaison.

**Mandatory liaisons:** After *les, des, mes, tes, ses, nos, vos, leurs, ces* + vowel; after *un, en, on* + vowel; after *très, bien, trop* + vowel.

**Forbidden liaisons:** After *et* (and), after a singular noun, after proper names ending in consonant.

**The *d* → *t* rule:** In liaison, *d* is pronounced *t*: *un grand arbre* → [grɑ̃.taʁbʁ]. This explains the spelling of *quand il* → [kɑ̃.til]: the *d* becomes *t* in liaison.

**The cultural note:** Liaison is one of the French phonetic features that most separates native speakers from learners. The obligatory liaisons must be made; the forbidden liaisons must be avoided; the optional liaisons are calibrated by register (more liaisons = more formal speech). Native speakers don't think about this — they hear incorrect liaison as a wrong note.

---

### ORTH-05 — Final Silent Letters

**The rule:** French words often end in written letters that are not pronounced.

*grand* [gʁɑ̃] — the *d* is silent (but heard in liaison: *grand ami*)
*paris* — the *s* is silent (but heard in liaison sometimes)
*vous parlez* — the *z* ending is silent
*ils parlent* — the *-ent* ending is silent

**Why silent letters exist:**
1. **Latin fossils:** Latin *grandis* → French *grand*. The *d* is preserved in spelling but lost in pronunciation.
2. **Historical agreement markers:** The *-ent* ending of third person plural was once pronounced; phonetic erosion removed the sound while spelling preserved the historical form.
3. **Liaison insurance:** Silent consonants that participate in liaison are preserved because they are phonetically active in certain environments.

**The practical rule:** French is read with its spelling as a guide to history. What you see is the word's DNA, not always its living sound.

---

### ORTH-06 — The *S* Pronunciation Rules

**The rule:** *S* is pronounced [s] at the start of a word or after a consonant. Between two vowels, *s* is pronounced [z].

*salon* [s] — initial *s*
*sel* [s] — initial *s*
*rose* [z] — *s* between vowels
*maison* [z] — *s* between vowels
*poisson* [s] — double *s* between vowels = [s]
*poison* [z] — single *s* between vowels = [z]

The difference between *poisson* (fish) and *poison* (poison) is one *s*. Both words for important French concepts (gastronomy and murder).

## A2 — PLURALS AND AGREEMENTS

---

### ORTH-07 — Plural Formation: The Standard and the Exceptions

**Standard:** Add *-s* (usually silent): *maison → maisons, livre → livres*.

**-S, -X, -Z endings:** No change in plural (already ends in a sibilant): *voix → voix, bras → bras, nez → nez*.

**-AU, -EAU, -EU → -AUX, -EAUX, -EUX:** *bateau → bateaux, jeu → jeux, feu → feux*.

**-AL → -AUX:** *journal → journaux, animal → animaux, cheval → chevaux*.
But exceptions: *bal → bals, carnaval → carnavals, festival → festivals*.

**-AIL → -AILS or -AUX:** Mixed: *travail → travaux, détail → détails, rail → rails*.

**Note on pronunciation:** The plural *-s* is silent in most environments but pronounced in liaison: *les enfants* [le.zɑ̃.fɑ̃].

---

### ORTH-08 — Feminine Adjective Formation: What the Ending Hides

**Standard:** Add *-e* to masculine: *grand → grande, petit → petite*.

When adding *-e* makes a final consonant suddenly audible:
- *grand* [gʁɑ̃] → *grande* [gʁɑ̃d] — the *d* is now heard
- *petit* [p(ə).ti] → *petite* [p(ə).tit] — the *t* is now heard

This is why written feminine forms *reveal* the masculine's hidden consonant.

**Doubled consonants:** *bon → bonne; cruel → cruelle; muet → muette; net → nette* — certain endings double the final consonant before *-e*.

**Irregular:** *beau/belle, nouveau/nouvelle, vieux/vieille, blanc/blanche, doux/douce, faux/fausse, long/longue, public/publique*.

---

### ORTH-09 — Past Participle Agreement Rules

**Rule 1 — With *être*:** Past participle agrees with subject:
*Elle est partie. Ils sont arrivés. Elles se sont levées.*

**Rule 2 — With *avoir*:** Past participle agrees with a preceding direct object:
*La lettre qu'il a écrite* (the letter that he wrote — *la lettre* is the preceding direct object, feminine singular → *écrite*)
*Les lettres qu'il a écrites* (plural *lettres* → *écrites*)
But: *Il a écrit une lettre* (no agreement — direct object follows the verb)

**Rule 3 — Reflexive verbs:** The reflexive pronoun often (not always) functions as the direct object, triggering agreement: *Elle s'est coiffée* (she did her hair — *se* = direct object). But: *Elle s'est lavé les mains* (no agreement — *les mains* is the direct object, not *se*).

**The cultural note:** Past participle agreement is one of the most complex orthographic rules in French, and one of the most discussed. The 1990 reforms proposed simplifying some cases; the Académie has resisted. In practice, many French people get these agreements wrong in informal writing without social consequence — the agreements are primarily a marker of formal written competence.

---

### ORTH-10 — Homophones: Sound-Alike, Write-Different

French has extensive homophone pairs — words that sound identical but are written differently:

| Sound | Words | Meanings |
|---|---|---|
| [a] | *a / à* | has / at |
| [ɛ] | *est / et* | is / and |
| [se] | *c'est / s'est / ces / ses* | it is / has + self / these / his/her |
| [o] | *au / eau / haut* | to the / water / high |
| [sɔ̃] | *son / sont* | his/her / are |
| [vu] | *vous / vou* | you / — |
| [vɛʁ] | *vers / vert / ver / verre* | toward / green / worm / glass |
| [sɑ̃] | *sang / cent / sans / sens* | blood / hundred / without / meaning |

The *vers/vert/ver/verre* quadruplet is a favourite example: written differently, all [vɛʁ]. French spelling distinguishes what French pronunciation conflates. The written language is a disambiguation system.

## B1 — CONJUGATION SPELLING AND REFORM

---

### ORTH-11 — Verb Spelling Changes in *-er* Verbs

Several groups of *-er* verbs change their spelling (but not their pronunciation structure) in certain conjugations:

**E → È before mute syllable:** *acheter → j'achète, tu achètes, il achète* (but *nous achetons*).
**L or T doubled before mute syllable:** *appeler → j'appelle; jeter → je jette* (but *nous appelons, nous jetons*).
**C → Ç before A, O, U:** *commencer → nous commençons; placer → je place* (no cedilla before *e*).
**G → GE before A, O:** *manger → nous mangeons; voyager → je voyageais*.

**1990 reform:** *appeler* and *jeter* can now be spelled with accent grave instead of doubled consonant: *j'appèle* (was *j'appelle*), *je jète* (was *je jette*). The reform simplifies by regularizing. Both forms are accepted.

---

### ORTH-12 — The Grave Accent as a Disambiguation Tool

Four word pairs where the grave accent distinguishes meaning with no change in pronunciation:

| Unaccented | Meaning | Accented | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| *a* | has (verb) | *à* | at/to (preposition) |
| *ou* | or | *où* | where |
| *la* | the (f.) / her | *là* | there |
| *du* | some/of the | (no *dù*) | — |

Why the accent? To distinguish homophones in writing — the orthographic system compensates for phonetic ambiguity. The accent grave on *à* is purely semantic, not phonetic.

---

### ORTH-13 — The Silent *H*: Historical Presence

**Aspirated vs Mute H (revisited at B1):**

The French *h* has two distinct behaviors, but both varieties are phonetically silent. The difference is phonological (it affects liaison and elision) and historical:

- *H muet* (mute): from Latin words where the *h* was also absent or mute. *Homme* (from Latin *homo*), *heure* (from Latin *hora*), *histoire* (from Latin *historia*). Elision and liaison occur: *l'homme, les z-hommes*.

- *H aspiré* (aspirated): often from Germanic borrowings where the *h* was once pronounced. *Haricot* (Germanic origin — *haricot* vs *l'haricot* — contested; traditionally *h aspiré*, so *le haricot*, not *l'haricot*). *Hibou* (owl), *haie* (hedge). No elision; no liaison.

**Practical consequence:** Before *h aspiré* words, use the full form of articles and pronouns: *le haricot* (not *l'haricot*); *les haricots* (not linked *les z-haricots*).

This is one of the most reliable markers of native-level French — knowing which words take *h aspiré* behavior is a largely memorized competence.

---

### ORTH-14 — Hyphenation Rules

**Standard rule:** Compound words may be hyphenated, written as one word, or written as two words. The rules are complex and the 1990 reforms simplified some cases.

**Always hyphenated:** Numbers from 17 to 99 (compound): *dix-sept, vingt-trois, quatre-vingt-dix-neuf*. *Vingt et un, trente et un* (no hyphen when *et* is used).

**Hyphenated compounds:** *arc-en-ciel, chef-d'œuvre, avant-garde, vis-à-vis, c'est-à-dire, après-demain*.

**1990 reform:** Many hyphenated words were joined: *porte-monnaie → portemonnaie, week-end → weekend*. Both spellings are accepted; the joined form is the simplified standard.

## B2 — ADVANCED AGREEMENT AND REGISTER

---

### ORTH-15 — The Invariable *Tout* and Its Exceptions

*Tout* behaves differently depending on its grammatical function:

- **Adjective** (agrees): *tout le monde* (all the world — m.s.), *toute la semaine* (f.s.), *tous les jours* (m.pl.), *toutes les semaines* (f.pl.)
- **Adverb** (invariable, in theory): *elle est tout heureuse* (she is entirely happy) — BUT before feminine adjectives beginning with a consonant or *h aspiré*: *tout* becomes *toute*: *elle est toute petite* (she is all small), *il est tout petit* (invariable for masculine before consonant — but *toute petite* for feminine before consonant — one of French grammar's most discussed exceptions)
- **Pronoun** (agrees): *tous sont partis* (all [m.pl.] have left); *toutes sont parties* (all [f.pl.] have left)

The *tout* case reveals how French grammar balances phonetic rules (the final *t* is heard before vowels, silent before consonants) against orthographic agreement rules (adjectives agree).

---

### ORTH-16 — Agreement of Color Adjectives

**Simple color adjectives agree normally:** *une robe rouge, des chaussures rouges, un manteau blanc, une veste blanche*.

**Exceptions:**
1. **Color adjectives derived from nouns are invariable:** *une veste marron* (chestnut), *des chaussures orange, des yeux noisette* (hazel), *une robe bordeaux, des gants crème*. Because these are originally nouns (the color *orange* is named for the fruit *orange*), they remain invariable.
2. **Compound color adjectives are invariable:** *des yeux bleu-vert, une robe vert foncé* (dark green — *foncé* agrees with nothing because the whole phrase modifies *robe* as a compound).

**The cultural note:** French color vocabulary is extraordinarily rich — influenced by textile production (Lyon silk trade), painting (the Académie royale de peinture established color nomenclature), and heraldry (which uses a specific vocabulary: *azur, gueules, or, argent, sable, sinople*). The color agreement rules reveal the tension between adjective grammar and noun-derived vocabulary.

---

### ORTH-17 — Verbal Homophone Traps: Present vs Infinitive

**Common traps:**

*Vous pensez* (you think — present indicative, 2nd person plural) vs *penser* (to think — infinitive)
*Ils mangent* [mɑ̃ʒ] vs *manger* [mɑ̃ʒe] — slightly different sounds, but easily confused

**The big trap:** *-er vs -é vs -ez*

All three can sound identical in standard French:
- *manger* (infinitive)
- *mangé* (past participle: eaten)
- *mangez* (2nd person plural: you eat)

**The rule:** If you can replace the verb with a *-re* verb (*vendre, prendre*) in the sentence and the sentence still works, use the infinitive (*manger*, *vendre*). If *vendu* (past participle of *vendre*) works, use *mangé*. If *vendez* works, use *mangez*.

*Il faut **manger** (vendre) des légumes* — infinitive after *il faut*
*Vous **mangez** (vendez) trop vite* — present after *vous*
*Il a **mangé** (vendu) ses légumes* — past participle after auxiliary

---

### ORTH-18 — The Tréma and Its 1990 Reform

**Traditional tréma:** Used to separate adjacent vowel sounds: *naïf, haïr, Noël, Israël*.

**1990 reform:** The tréma moves to the second of two vowels when that vowel is a *u* that was mute in the traditional spelling but should be pronounced:

*ambiguë → ambigüe* (the *u* is now marked — before, the tréma was on the *e*; under the reform, it moves to the *u* to show it is pronounced: *ambiguë* [ɑ̃.bi.gy])
*aiguë → aigüe*

This reform affects a small number of words but has been controversial because it inverts the traditional placement of the tréma.

## C1 — REGISTER, PUNCTUATION, AND STYLE

---

### ORTH-19 — French Quotation Marks: Les Guillemets

**The rule:** French uses *guillemets français* (French quotation marks) ‹ ›: **« »**

*Il a dit : « Je ne sais pas. »*

**Space rules:** A space (in digital typography: a non-breaking thin space) is required between the guillemet and the quoted text:
*« texte »* — space after « and before ».

**Nested quotations:** For a quotation within a quotation, use double guillemets → single guillemets or English marks:
*« Il a dit : "Je ne sais pas", mais je ne l'ai pas cru. »*

**In dialogue:** French literary dialogue uses *tiret cadratin* (em dash) at the start of each speaker's line, without closing punctuation within the dialogue:
*— Je ne sais pas, dit-il.
— Mais si, tu sais, répondit-elle.*

---

### ORTH-20 — The Colon, Semicolon, and Space Rules

**The rule in French (formal register):** Certain punctuation marks require a space before them in French that is not used in English:

| Mark | English | French |
|---|---|---|
| Colon (:) | no space before | space before: *Je dis : oui.* |
| Semicolon (;) | no space before | space before: *Il vient ; elle reste.* |
| Question mark (?) | no space before | space before: *Comment ? Quoi ?* |
| Exclamation mark (!) | no space before | space before: *Bravo !* |
| Guillemets (« ») | n/a | space within: *« texte »* |

**In digital typography:** The spaces are technically *espaces insécables* (non-breaking spaces) — they prevent the punctuation from appearing at the start of a new line.

**In informal writing:** These spaces are routinely omitted in email and text messages. In formal documents, they are required.

---

### ORTH-21 — Elision Traps: Words That Look Like They Should Elide But Don't

**H aspiré words that prevent elision** (representative list):
*le hibou* (owl), *la honte* (shame), *le héros* (hero — *le héros* not *l'héros*, though *l'héroïne* — the feminine — allows elision), *le haricot* (bean), *la honte*, *le handball*, *le hublot* (porthole), *la Hollande*.

The rule has no simple phonetic basis for predicting *h aspiré* — it must be learned. Native speakers feel which words don't allow elision; learners must memorize or look it up.

**Exceptional non-elision:** *le onze novembre* (November 11th — *le* does not elide before *onze* or *onzième*, uniquely among numbers).

---

### ORTH-22 — Verb Forms That Look Identical

Three verb forms that share the same written form but differ in grammatical function:

1. **First singular passé simple = Second singular passé simple:** *Je parlai / tu parlas / il parla* — not the same, but *tu fus / je fus* for *être* are different.

2. **Third singular present of *-re* verbs = Third singular passé simple of *-er* verbs:**
*il rend* (present: he returns) vs *il parla* (passé simple: he spoke). Not confusable across verbs, but *il mord* (bites, present) vs *il mordit* (bit, passé simple) require tense awareness.

3. **Subjunctive present = Present indicative for many verbs:**
*je parle* (indicative) = *que je parle* (subjunctive) — visually identical, grammatically different. The subjunctive is distinguished by context (triggered by subjunctive-requiring expressions) rather than form.

## C2 — LITERARY ORTHOGRAPHY AND THE ACADEMY

---

### ORTH-23 — Les Rectifications Orthographiques de 1990 — The Full Picture

The 1990 reforms affected approximately 2,400 words. Key changes:

**1. Circumflex on *i* and *u*:** Eliminated in most words where it had no semantic function.
*paraître → paraître* (retained: distinguishes *il paraît* from *il parait* — reform allows no accent), *maître → maitre, traître → traitre, boîte → boite, île → ile, coût → cout, goût → gout, voûte → voute*

**Exception — kept for disambiguation:** The circumflex is retained when it distinguishes homophones: *du* (article) vs *dû* (past participle of *devoir*); *sur* (preposition) vs *sûr* (certain); *cru* (past participle) vs *crû* (past participle of *croître*).

**2. Compound nouns with plural:** Many compound nouns simplified their plural forms.
*un coupe-vent* → plural *des coupe-vents* (not *des coupe-vent*).

**3. Anglicisms:** Foreign words regularized in French plurals.
*un maximum → des maximums* (not *des maxima* — the Latin plural is replaced by a French one).

**4. Numbers:** Hyphens now connect all elements of compound numbers:
*vingt et un → vingt-et-un* (the 1990 reform adds the hyphen; *cent vingt et un → cent vingt-et-un*).

**The controversy:** The 1990 reforms were technically approved by the Académie but never fully adopted by French teachers or publishers. The situation is that both spellings are officially accepted, but many teachers continue to mark the old spellings as the standard. The learner should know both and be prepared to defend either.

---

### ORTH-24 — The Académie Française and Orthographic Authority

**The institution:** The Académie française, founded 1635, has 40 members (*les Immortels*) who maintain the *Dictionnaire de l'Académie française* (9th edition, ongoing since 1986). The Dictionary is the official reference for French spelling; its rulings are binding for the French state.

**The limits of authority:** The Académie's rulings are advisory for speakers outside France. Belgian, Swiss, Quebecois, and West African French have their own orthographic conventions in some areas. The *Office québécois de la langue française* publishes its own orthographic guidance, sometimes differing from the Académie.

**Contested rulings:**
- **Gender-inclusive writing (*écriture inclusive*):** The Académie issued a strongly negative opinion in 2021 against the use of the *point médian* in plural forms (*les étudiant·e·s*). The government has restricted its use in official documents. The debate continues.
- **Feminization of titles:** *auteure, autrice, écrivaine, chercheuse* — once resisted, now accepted in the 9th edition Dictionary and widely used.
- **Anglicisms:** The Académie proposes French alternatives (*courriel* for *email*, *mèl*) that French speakers largely ignore. *Email* is universal in usage.

**The learner's position:** Use the Académie's standard for formal written French. Be aware that usage diverges. Know the difference between the written standard and the living language.

---

### ORTH-25 — Le Passé Simple Orthography: Where Accents Make History

The passé simple uses circumflex accents in first and second person plural — one of the few cases where circumflexes are productive in modern French:

*nous parlâmes, vous parlâtes* — the circumflex marks the passé simple
*nous avons parlé* — no circumflex in passé composé

*nous finîmes, vous finîtes*
*nous vînmes, vous vîntes* (from *venir*)
*nous fûmes, vous fûtes* (from *être*)
*nous eûmes, vous eûtes* (from *avoir*)

The circumflex in passé simple forms is retained even under the 1990 reforms — because in these forms, it has a disambiguating function: *nous chantâmes* (passé simple) vs *nous chantons* (present) vs *nous chantions* (imparfait). The accent marks the tense.

---

### ORTH-26 — Orthography as Cultural Identity

**The final rule:** French spelling is not a neutral technical system. It is a cultural position.

To spell *hôpital* with a circumflex is to participate in the historical French tradition that preserved the memory of Latin *hospitale* through the spelling conventions of the medieval scriptorium and the Renaissance printer. To spell it *hopital* (the reformed form) is to privilege usability over historical memory.

Neither choice is wrong. The choice reveals a relationship to the language's past.

The French have argued about spelling for four hundred years — since Ronsard and Du Bellay's *Défense et illustration de la langue française* (1549), which launched the argument for writing French according to its sounds rather than its Latin origins. The argument has never been resolved because it cannot be: French spelling is simultaneously a phonetic guide, a historical record, and a social filter. To master it completely is to have mastered all three at once.

**The learner who reaches this point has done something remarkable.** They have not just learned to spell in French. They have learned the archaeology of a civilization embedded in its written forms.
