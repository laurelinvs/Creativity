# B2–C2 Lesson Plans
## Professionnel · Stratégique · Maîtrise

> *"Le style, c'est l'homme même."*
> — Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon, 1753

These three levels are where language becomes power. At B2, the learner operates in professional and formal registers. At C1, they persuade, ironize, and argue with precision. At C2, they have what Buffon described: the style that is indistinguishable from the self — the voice that is entirely theirs, expressed in another language. These lesson plans provide the teaching arc, vocabulary, grammar, and cultural depth for each of the thirty remaining lessons.

# B2 — PROFESSIONNEL
## Operating With Precision and Register Awareness

The B2 learner speaks French without survival anxiety. What they are learning now is *register* — the ability to calibrate language for context. The same message delivered differently to a client, a colleague, a minister, or a friend. B2 is where French becomes a professional instrument.

---

## Lesson 1 — Le Monde Professionnel: Career, Ambition, and the CV

**Central Insight:** French professional communication has a formality architecture unlike most other cultures — the wrong register can close a door that skill and qualification would have opened.

**Cultural Thread:**
The *grandes écoles* system — Sciences Po, HEC, Polytechnique, ENA (now INSP) — produces approximately 5% of French graduates and fills 80% of senior leadership positions in the state, major corporations, and politics. The *énarque* (graduate of ENA/INSP) was, for sixty years, France's governing class — presidents, prime ministers, ministers, prefects, CEOs. This system creates an elite that shares not just training but a precise linguistic register: the language of the *rapport*, the *note de synthèse*, and the *compte-rendu*. To function at a B2 professional level in French is to learn this register without necessarily graduating from it.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *La Carrière — Language as Professional Instrument*
"In France, how you say it often matters more than what you say. The wrong opening in a cover letter, the wrong honorific in an email, the wrong register at a meeting — each is a social signal. Knowing the code is not sycophancy. It is competence."

*Screen 2 — Professional Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **le cadre** — executive, managerial employee
- **le poste de direction** — management position
- **la hiérarchie** — hierarchy (a French workplace fixture)
- **le PDG** (*Président-Directeur Général*) — CEO
- **le DRH** (*Directeur des Ressources Humaines*) — HR Director
- **les ressources humaines** — HR
- **la fiche de poste** — job description
- **la mobilité** — professional mobility (moving roles/locations)

*Screen 3 — Register at Work (concept)*
Three registers coexist in French professional life:
- **Formel écrit** (formal written): *Veuillez agréer..., je vous prie de bien vouloir..., en référence à votre courrier...*
- **Formel oral** (formal spoken): *J'aimerais attirer votre attention sur..., permettez-moi de...*
- **Professionnel courant** (everyday professional): *Je reviens vers vous, je fais le point, on en reparle*

*Screen 4 — The Professional Email (concept)*
French email etiquette:
- Opening: **Madame, Monsieur,** (comma after, separate line) or **Bonjour Madame Dupont,**
- Body: clear, formal, no contractions
- Closing: **Cordialement,** (standard) / **Bien cordialement,** (warmer) / **Salutations distinguées,** (very formal) / **Avec mes meilleures salutations,**
- NEVER: **Cher/Chère** in a professional email to someone you don't know well. This is intimate register.

*Screen 5 — Writing Formulas (vocabulary)*
- **Je me permets de vous contacter...** — I take the liberty of contacting you...
- **Suite à notre entretien du...** — Following our meeting on...
- **En réponse à votre annonce...** — In response to your job posting...
- **Je reste à votre disposition pour...** — I remain available for...
- **Dans l'attente de votre retour...** — Awaiting your response...
- **Je vous souhaite une bonne réception de ce message.** — (formal close: wishing them a good receipt of the message)

*Screen 6 — Meetings and Presentations (vocabulary)*
- **un compte-rendu** — minutes/meeting report
- **un ordre du jour** — agenda (*ordre du jour* literally = "order of the day" — originally a parliamentary term from the French Revolution)
- **une prise de parole** — a speech/floor contribution
- **prendre la parole** — to take the floor
- **la synthèse** — the summary/synthesis
- **un rapport** — a report
- **une note de service** — a memo

*Screen 7 — Giving a Presentation (examples)*
- **Je vais vous présenter les résultats du trimestre.** — I'm going to present the quarterly results.
- **Commençons par...** — Let's begin with...
- **J'en viens maintenant à...** — I now come to...
- **Pour conclure...** — To conclude...
- **Je vous invite à poser vos questions.** — I invite you to ask your questions.

*Screen 8 — Negotiation Language (vocabulary)*
- **un compromis** — a compromise
- **une concession** — a concession
- **une contrepartie** — a counterpart/concession (*contre* + *partie*)
- **les termes du contrat** — the contract terms
- **une clause** — a clause
- **renégocier** — to renegotiate
- **une offre ferme** — a firm offer

*Screen 9 — Cultural Note: The French Meeting (narrative)*
French meetings (*les réunions*) are not decision-making events. Decisions are typically made beforehand, in corridors, in individual conversations. The meeting is for formal consensus, for hierarchy to be visible, for dissent to be heard (and noted). Coming to a French meeting and expecting a decision to emerge from collective discussion can be frustrating. The French word *réunion* comes from *réunir* — to reunite — suggesting that the meeting is primarily a social act: the gathering of people who share a project, not the mechanism by which that project advances.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- Three registers: formel écrit, formel oral, professionnel courant.
- Email: *Madame, Monsieur,* opening; *Cordialement* or *Salutations distinguées* close.
- **Never** *Cher/Chère* to professional strangers.
- French meetings are consensus confirmation, not decision engines.
- The *grandes écoles* register shapes all formal French professional language.

**Grammar Point:** Nominalization — Turning Verbs into Nouns
French formal register prefers noun phrases to verb phrases:
- *nous allons analyser* → *une analyse de*...
- *la situation s'améliore* → *une amélioration de la situation*
- *on a décidé* → *suite à la décision de*...
Nominalization makes writing denser, more formal, and more difficult to challenge — which is why bureaucratic and legal French is so heavily nominalized. At B2, recognizing and producing nominalization is a register marker.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Je reviens vers vous* — "I'll get back to you" — is the corporate French phrase that has colonized every professional email. It comes from the hunting metaphor *revenir vers* (to come back toward) and entered business French in the 1990s alongside email culture. It is everywhere. Using it correctly places you inside the French professional ecosystem. Using *je vous recontacte* is equivalent but slightly less polished.

## Lesson 2 — L'Argent et la Finance: Money, Banking, and the French Economic Mind

**Central Insight:** France has a complicated, historically loaded relationship with money — the country that invented the welfare state also considers discussing one's salary deeply rude.

**Cultural Thread:**
France has both a deep Protestant ethic of saving (*l'épargne* — a virtue more than a habit) and an aristocratic disdain for open money talk. The *nouvelle cuisine* of finance, like the Revolution itself, is complicated: France's major banks (BNP Paribas, Crédit Agricole, Société Générale) are among Europe's largest; yet French salary discussions are taboo in a way they are not in the US or UK. You do not ask a French colleague what they earn. This reticence is class-coded: the bourgeoisie historically did not discuss money in public because discussing it acknowledged its centrality — and the old money preferred to suggest that money was not the point.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *L'Argent — The Thing That Must Not Be Named*
"France built one of the world's most comprehensive welfare states on taxes. Its citizens are among Europe's highest-taxed. And its social code forbids discussing money at dinner. Both facts are French."

*Screen 2 — Banking Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **un compte bancaire** — a bank account
- **un compte courant** — a current account (*courant* = current, flowing)
- **un livret A** — the regulated French savings account (interest rate fixed by government; held by 80% of French people)
- **un virement** — a bank transfer
- **un prélèvement automatique** — a direct debit
- **un chèque** — a cheque (still widely used in France — more than in most European countries)
- **le RIB** (*Relevé d'Identité Bancaire*) — bank account details card (required for almost every French administrative transaction)

*Screen 3 — Finance Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **les impôts** — taxes (income tax)
- **la TVA** (*Taxe sur la Valeur Ajoutée*) — VAT (currently 20% standard rate)
- **le budget** — budget
- **les dépenses** — expenditure
- **les recettes** — income/revenue
- **la dette** — debt
- **la croissance** — economic growth
- **la récession** — recession

*Screen 4 — The French Tax System (cultural slide)*
The French income tax (*l'impôt sur le revenu*) was only introduced in 1914, shortly before WWI — remarkably late for a modern state. For the previous century, France ran largely on indirect taxes (customs, excise). The withholding system (*prélèvement à la source*) — where tax is deducted directly from salary like in most countries — was only introduced in France in 2019. Before that, for 105 years, French people paid their income tax a year in arrears via a form they submitted themselves. This extraordinary system is now changed, but it explains why the French have a more complex, active relationship with their tax declarations than most Europeans.

*Screen 5 — Salary Talk (concept)*
French salary norms:
- **le SMIC** (*Salaire Minimum Interprofessionnel de Croissance*) — the minimum wage (indexed to inflation)
- **la grille de salaires** — salary scale
- **une augmentation** — a pay rise
- **une prime** — a bonus
- **les charges** — social contributions (employer contributions on top of gross salary — can be 40-50% of salary cost)
- *Ne jamais demander le salaire de quelqu'un* — never ask someone their salary; this is the most reliable social taboo in French professional culture.

*Screen 6 — Discussing Economy and Finance (examples)*
- **Le gouvernement a annoncé une baisse des impôts.** — The government announced a tax cut.
- **La croissance économique est estimée à 1,5% cette année.** — Economic growth is estimated at 1.5% this year.
- **Les marchés financiers réagissent à l'incertitude politique.** — Financial markets are reacting to political uncertainty.
- **Il faut équilibrer le budget sans augmenter la dette.** — The budget must be balanced without increasing the debt.

*Screen 7 — Formal Connectors at B2 (concept)*
At B2, opinions gain precision through formal connectors:
- **En effet** — indeed (confirms what preceded; more formal than *oui*)
- **Ainsi** — thus (logical consequence)
- **Par conséquent** — consequently
- **Toutefois** — however (more formal than *mais*)
- **Dès lors** — from that point on / therefore (if...then structure)
- **Force est de constater que** — one cannot but note that (strong formal observation)
- **Il convient de souligner que** — it is appropriate to emphasize that

*Screen 8 — The French Relationship with Risk (narrative)*
France's cultural preference for *la sécurité* (security) over *l'entrepreneuriat* (entrepreneurship) has been documented and debated. The *fonctionnaire* ideal — the civil servant with a permanent contract (*titulaire*), healthcare, pension, and guaranteed employment — has historically attracted more talent than the startup. This is changing: France's tech sector (*la French Tech*, the government brand for the startup ecosystem, centered in Paris's *Station F* — the world's largest startup campus) is now significant. But the cultural preference for *la sécurité de l'emploi* (job security) over *la prise de risque* (risk-taking) remains visible in career choices.

*Screen 9 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Pourriez-vous me faire parvenir un RIB pour procéder au virement ?** — Could you send me a bank account form to process the transfer?
- **Notre budget prévisionnel pour l'an prochain s'élève à deux millions d'euros.** — Our projected budget for next year amounts to two million euros.
- **Les charges sociales représentent environ 45% du coût total pour l'employeur.** — Social contributions represent approximately 45% of the total cost for the employer.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- *Le RIB* is required for almost every French financial transaction. Keep it accessible.
- *Le SMIC* is the indexed minimum wage — regularly adjusted.
- Discussing someone's salary is the most reliable French social taboo.
- France adopted payroll tax withholding (*prélèvement à la source*) only in 2019.
- *La French Tech* and *Station F* are France's entrepreneurial response to the fonctionnaire ideal.

**Grammar Point:** Complex Sentence Construction — Nominalization and Subordination
B2 requires sentences like: *Suite à la décision prise lors de la réunion du 12 mars, il a été convenu que les parties procéderaient à une révision des termes contractuels d'ici la fin du trimestre.*
Elements: nominalization (*la décision prise*), passive (*il a été convenu*), subordinate clause (*que les parties procéderaient*), conditional implied by sequence-of-tenses.
This is legal-professional register. Practice parsing it before producing it.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Force est de constater* — one cannot but note — is one of French formal writing's most useful formulas. It signals that the following observation is inescapable, not the speaker's preference but the evidence's conclusion. Politicians use it. Journalists use it. It projects objectivity while making an argument. Once you recognize it in a French text, you will see it everywhere.

## Lesson 3 — La Politique: Governance, Institutions, and the Fifth Republic

**Central Insight:** France has had five republics, two empires, three monarchies, and one occupation government since 1789. The current republic's constitution was written by one man for one man: de Gaulle, in 1958.

**Cultural Thread:**
The Fifth Republic, founded in 1958, is a semi-presidential system designed to solve the parliamentary instability of the Fourth Republic (21 governments in 12 years). De Gaulle's constitution gave enormous powers to the president — elected directly by universal suffrage since 1962, for a five-year term renewable once. The president appoints the prime minister, chairs the cabinet, and can dissolve the National Assembly and call a referendum. France is neither a pure presidential system (the government must retain parliamentary confidence) nor a pure parliamentary one. The cohabitation period (1986-88, 1993-95, 1997-2002 — when president and prime minister were from opposing parties) revealed the system's tensions without breaking it.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *La République — Power Written Into Law*
"De Gaulle created a constitution designed to produce strong government. He also included Article 16, giving the president emergency powers 'when the institutions of the Republic are seriously threatened.' It has been invoked once: by de Gaulle himself, in 1961."

*Screen 2 — Political Institutions (vocabulary)*
- **l'Élysée** — the presidential palace (*le palais de l'Élysée* = office/residence of the President)
- **Matignon** — the Prime Minister's office/residence (*l'Hôtel Matignon*)
- **l'Assemblée nationale** — the National Assembly (lower house, 577 deputies, elected every 5 years)
- **le Sénat** — the Senate (upper house, 348 senators, indirectly elected)
- **le Conseil constitutionnel** — the Constitutional Council (9 members; rules on constitutionality of laws)
- **le Conseil d'État** — the Council of State (highest administrative court; also advises government on legislation)

*Screen 3 — Political Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **une élection** — an election
- **le suffrage universel** — universal suffrage
- **un(e) député(e)** — a member of the National Assembly
- **un(e) sénateur/sénatrice** — a senator
- **un ministre** — a minister
- **le gouvernement** — the government (the cabinet, not the broader state)
- **l'opposition** — the opposition
- **un parti politique** — a political party

*Screen 4 — The French Political Spectrum (concept)*
French political vocabulary has its own topology:
- **la gauche** — the left (historically: Socialist, Communist, Green, now also La France Insoumise)
- **la droite** — the right (historically: Gaullist, now: Les Républicains, Rassemblement National at the far right)
- **le centre** — the center (*En Marche*/Renaissance — Macron's movement)
- **l'extrême gauche** — far left
- **l'extrême droite** — far right (*le RN*, formerly Front National)
- **les Insoumis** — La France Insoumise (radical left, Jean-Luc Mélenchon)
- **les Verts** — the Greens (*Europe Écologie Les Verts*)

*Screen 5 — Political Discourse Phrases (concept)*
- **mettre en œuvre une réforme** — to implement a reform
- **voter une loi** — to pass a law
- **promulguer** — to promulgate (presidential act that makes a law official)
- **la motion de censure** — the no-confidence motion
- **le 49-3** — Article 49.3 of the Constitution — allows the government to pass legislation without a vote, triggering automatic no-confidence challenge. Used controversially for pension reform.
- **le couvre-feu** — curfew (entered vocabulary during the 2020 COVID crisis)

*Screen 6 — Referenda and Direct Democracy (cultural slide)*
France has used the referendum for major constitutional questions: joining the European Community (1972), creating the Senate and Regional Councils (1969 — de Gaulle lost and immediately resigned), ratifying the EU Constitution (2005 — the French voted *Non*, which shocked Europe and killed the treaty). The French relationship with direct democracy is ambivalent: they theoretically believe in it but frequently vote to reject whatever the government wants them to approve. The *gilets jaunes* (2018-2019) demanded a *Référendum d'Initiative Citoyenne* (RIC) — citizens' initiative referendum. It was not granted.

*Screen 7 — Political Analysis (examples)*
- **Le gouvernement a engagé sa responsabilité sur ce projet de loi.** — The government has staked its responsibility on this bill. (used the 49-3)
- **L'opposition a déposé une motion de censure.** — The opposition filed a no-confidence motion.
- **Les sondages donnent le parti au pouvoir en difficulté.** — Polls show the governing party in difficulty.
- **La cohabitation a contraint le président à une politique de compromis.** — Cohabitation forced the president toward compromise policy.

*Screen 8 — Abstentionism (narrative)*
French electoral abstentionism (*l'abstentionnisme*) is a genuine political crisis. In the 2022 legislative elections, 53% of eligible voters abstained — the highest rate ever. This is interpreted variously as political disengagement, protest, or structural fatigue with a political class perceived as disconnected from lived reality. The *vote blanc* (blank vote) is officially counted in France since 2014 — the state recognizes the act of voting without choosing as a democratic act. This is characteristically French: the refusal to choose is itself a choice, and the Republic respects it enough to count it.

*Screen 9 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Le Président a annoncé la dissolution de l'Assemblée nationale.** — The President announced the dissolution of the National Assembly.
- **Cette réforme, si elle est adoptée, modifiera profondément l'architecture sociale du pays.** — If adopted, this reform will profoundly modify the country's social architecture.
- **Le débat parlementaire a révélé de profondes divisions au sein de la majorité.** — The parliamentary debate revealed deep divisions within the majority.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- Fifth Republic (1958): semi-presidential, strong executive, de Gaulle's design.
- *L'Élysée* = President. *Matignon* = Prime Minister.
- *L'Assemblée nationale* (elected, sovereign) + *le Sénat* (indirectly elected, less powerful).
- *Le 49-3*: the constitutional provision that allows government to bypass a vote.
- Abstentionism at 53%: a structural crisis of democratic engagement.

**Grammar Point:** *Si* + Plus-Que-Parfait → Conditionnel Passé
The past counterfactual:
*Si de Gaulle n'avait pas écrit cette constitution, la France aurait peut-être connu une sixième République.*
(If de Gaulle hadn't written this constitution, France might have experienced a sixth Republic.)
Form: *si + plus-que-parfait (avait/était + past participle) → conditionnel passé (aurait/serait + past participle)*
This expresses regret, hypothesis about the past, and counterfactual thinking — the highest register of hypothetical French.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Le 49-3* has become a household term in French. When a prime minister "passes the 49-3," they are bypassing parliamentary vote — legally and constitutionally — but at political cost. Elisabeth Borne's use of it 23 times during the pension reform debate of 2023 produced the largest wave of social protest in France since 1995. The constitutional article and the number "49-3" are now shorthand for executive overreach in common parlance. At B2, being able to use *le 49-3* in a sentence about French politics is a register marker.

## Lesson 4 — La Technologie: Digital France and the Digital Divide

**Central Insight:** France is simultaneously the world's leader in nuclear energy (75% of electricity), a major digital innovation hub (Mistral AI, Deezer, BlaBlaCar), and a country whose public administration still requires you to physically mail paper documents to the tax authority.

**Teaching Arc:** [Condensed plan]

Core vocabulary: *la transformation numérique, l'intelligence artificielle, les données personnelles, le RGPD (GDPR), la cybersécurité, les objets connectés (IoT), le cloud, le big data, une startup, une licorne (unicorn company), l'open source, le télétravail (remote work)*.

Cultural thread: France's *Minitel* (1980-2012) was the world's first mass internet — a video-text terminal in millions of homes before the World Wide Web existed. The French invented online banking, online train booking, and online dating (before the Americans) — on the Minitel. When the Web arrived, France was briefly behind because it had invested so deeply in the Minitel. The lesson: technological leadership is not permanent.

Grammar: The future simple for technology predictions (*dans dix ans, l'IA **remplacera** certains emplois*) — and the conditional for uncertainty (*elle **pourrait** transformer*).

Key cultural note: *RGPD* (the EU's General Data Protection Regulation) was largely written by French and German lawyers. The *CNIL* (Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés), France's data protection authority, is one of the world's most active. France takes digital privacy rights seriously at an institutional level while French citizens share their data freely on social media — a characteristic French tension between the state's protectiveness and individual behaviour.

## Lesson 5 — L'Environnement: Ecology, Climate, and the Green Republic

**Central Insight:** France hosted the Paris Agreement (2015) and is simultaneously building new nuclear reactors and phasing out coal. The country approaches ecology through its Republican universalism — climate change as a civilization threat, not just an environmental problem.

**Teaching Arc:** [Condensed plan]

Core vocabulary: *le réchauffement climatique, la transition énergétique, les énergies renouvelables, le nucléaire, le bilan carbone, la biodiversité, l'empreinte écologique, le développement durable, un éco-quartier, la sobriété énergétique, le tri sélectif (recycling)*.

Cultural thread: The French écologie movement has deep roots — Les Verts (the Greens) have been a significant political force since the 1970s. The *Rapport Meadows* (Limits to Growth, 1972) was translated into French and widely read. Nicolas Hulot, the television naturalist turned ecology minister (2017-2018), resigned live on France Inter radio, citing the impossibility of achieving ecological goals within the political system — one of the most dramatic resignations in French political history.

Grammar: The subjunctive of obligation and necessity: *Il est essentiel que nous **réduisions** nos émissions.* (It is essential that we reduce our emissions.) Verbs of obligation (*il faut que, il est nécessaire que, il est urgent que*) + subjonctif.

Key cultural note: The *Gilets Jaunes* movement began in 2018 as a revolt against a fuel tax increase justified on ecological grounds. The collision between environmental policy (affecting rural car users disproportionately) and social inequality revealed a fault line in French ecology: the ecological transition is perceived by some as a luxury of the urban educated classes, imposed on those who cannot afford electric vehicles or city centre apartments.

## Lesson 6 — Idées Abstraites: Philosophy in Everyday French

**Central Insight:** Abstract vocabulary is not reserved for intellectuals in France — it circulates in daily conversation, in radio debates, and at the dinner table.

**Teaching Arc:** [Condensed plan]

Core vocabulary: *la liberté, l'égalité, la fraternité, la solidarité, la justice, le droit, le devoir, la responsabilité, la conscience, l'identité, la mémoire collective, le vivre-ensemble (living together), l'altruisme, la communauté*.

Cultural thread: The French *dissertation* — the philosophical essay format taught from age 15 — structures how educated French people think. Every philosophical question has a *plan en trois parties*: the thesis, the antithesis, the synthesis. This structure is not just academic — it appears in political speeches, in newspaper editorials, in the *grande interview* of France Culture. Knowing how to move through this structure makes you sound not just fluent but intellectually grounded in French.

Grammar: The construction *Il s'agit de* (it is a matter of) — one of the most important formal expressions in French: *Il ne s'agit pas simplement de choisir entre deux options — il s'agit de définir ce que nous voulons être.* (It is not simply a matter of choosing between two options — it is a matter of defining what we want to be.)

Key cultural note: The French *intello* — the intellectual — is not a fringe figure. On French television (Apostrophes, La Grande Librairie, Ce soir ou jamais) and radio (France Culture's *Les Chemins de la philosophie*), philosophers, historians, and essayists have mainstream audiences. Bernard-Henri Lévy, Michel Houellebecq, and Virginie Despentes are cultural figures of the first order — discussed in magazines, at dinner parties, in political speeches.

## Lesson 7 — Le Registre: Formal vs Informal French

**Central Insight:** The gap between written formal French and spoken informal French is wider in French than in almost any other European language.

**Teaching Arc:** [Condensed plan]

Core concept: Three levels of language in French:
- **Soutenu** (formal/literary): *Je ne saurais vous le dire; nul ne saurait prévoir; il eût été préférable...*
- **Courant** (standard): *Je ne sais pas; personne ne peut prévoir; il aurait été préférable...*
- **Familier/Argot** (colloquial/slang): *J'sais pas; y'a personne qui peut savoir; c'était mieux d'...*

Examples of register shift:
- *Je vais manger* (standard) → *Je vais bouffer* (colloquial: to eat, from *boeuf* — to gobble like a bull)
- *Il est très beau* (standard) → *Il est grave beau* (verlan/youth register: *grave* = extremely)
- *Partir* (standard) → *se casser, se tailler, se barrer* (colloquial departures)

Cultural thread: *Le verlan* — back-slang, where syllables are reversed: *l'envers* (the reverse) → *verlan*. *Beur* (Arab) = verlan of *arabe*. *Meuf* = verlan of *femme* (woman). *Keum* = verlan of *mec* (guy). Developed in the *banlieues* in the 1980s, verlan became the sonic signature of French hip-hop and entered mainstream youth language.

Grammar: *Ne* dropping in spoken French — the most consistent marker of colloquial speech. *Je ne sais pas* → *je sais pas* (spoken). *Il ne vient pas* → *il vient pas*. The *ne* survives in writing and formal speech but is routinely dropped in everyday conversation. This is not incorrect — it is a recognized phonetic reduction.

## Lesson 8 — L'Argumentation: The French Essay and Persuasion

**Central Insight:** The French *dissertation* structure — thesis, antithesis, synthesis — is not an academic exercise. It is the structural DNA of French thought.

**Teaching Arc:** [Condensed plan]

Core vocabulary for argumentation:
*Premièrement / En premier lieu, Deuxièmement / Ensuite, Pour commencer, De plus, En outre, Certes / Il est vrai que, Cependant / Néanmoins / Toutefois, Or / Mais encore faut-il, En revanche, En définitive / En conclusion / Pour conclure / En guise de conclusion, Force est de constater, Quoi qu'il en soit, Il convient de souligner.*

Discourse structure: *l'annonce du plan* (announcing the essay plan — a distinctly French academic practice: explicitly stating what you will argue in which order at the start of an essay), *la problématique* (the central question to be resolved), *la thèse* (thesis), *l'antithèse* (antithesis), *la synthèse* (the synthesis or resolution).

Cultural thread: The French philosophy of argumentation — *la joute oratoire* (the verbal joust) — has ancient roots in the *disputatio* of medieval universities, where scholars formally argued opposing positions. French debate shows (like *Ce soir ou jamais*, *On n'est pas couchés*, *L'Émission politique*) still use this structure. The adversarial format is not aggression — it is dialectic.

Grammar: Advanced nominalization and abstraction in formal argument: not *beaucoup de gens pensent* but *un nombre significatif d'observateurs s'accorde à reconnaître que*; not *c'est un problème* but *une telle problématique ne saurait manquer de soulever*.

## Lesson 9 — Langue et Identité: French, Francophonie, and the Question of Belonging

**Central Insight:** French is not only France's language — it is the language of 300 million people across five continents, each community inflecting it with their own history, sounds, and cultural intelligence.

**Teaching Arc:** [Condensed plan]

Core vocabulary: *la francophonie, le français international, un créole, le français québécois, le français africain, un accent régional, une variante, un néologisme, l'OIF (Organisation Internationale de la Francophonie)*.

Key variants: Quebec French (*tire-toi une bûche* = pull up a chair; *char* = car; the *tu* interrogative: *t'as-tu mangé?*), Swiss French (*septante, huitante, nonante* for 70, 80, 90), Belgian French (*nonante; soixante* for 70; *un kot* = a student room), West African French (influenced by local languages, producing coinages like *sauver* = to log out, *décalé* = cool/relaxed).

Cultural thread: The Académie française — the guardian of French, founded 1635 — has 40 members (les Immortels) who maintain the Dictionary and rule on French usage. Its rulings are binding for the French state and advisory for everyone else. The Académie is politically and culturally conservative but has, since the late 20th century, included several francophone non-French members (Léopold Sédar Senghor from Senegal was elected in 1983 — the first African member). The paradox: the institution that polices the language is increasingly aware that it does not own it.

Grammar: Reported speech and tense changes: *il a dit qu'il **viendrait*** (he said he would come — present → conditional in reported speech); *elle a expliqué qu'elle **avait vu*** (she explained she had seen — passé composé → plus-que-parfait in reported).

## Lesson 10 — Les Nuances: Advanced Grammar for Precision

**Central Insight:** At B2, grammar is no longer survival equipment — it is an instrument of precision. The subjunctive, the plus-que-parfait, and the gérondif are the tools of a craftsperson, not a student.

**Teaching Arc:** [Condensed plan]

Focus: Four structures that mark B2 mastery:
1. **Le subjonctif dans la proposition relative**: *Je cherche quelqu'un qui **sache** le russe.* (I'm looking for someone who knows Russian — I don't know if they exist.) vs *J'ai trouvé quelqu'un qui **sait** le russe.* (I found someone who knows Russian — they exist.) The subjunctive expresses unrealized or hypothetical entities.

2. **Le gérondif**: *En étudiant chaque jour, vous progresserez.* — By studying every day, you will progress. Manner, condition, simultaneity — one invariable form.

3. **Le plus-que-parfait**: *Il avait déjà mangé quand elle est arrivée.* — He had already eaten when she arrived. The action further back in the past.

4. **L'infinitif passé**: *Après avoir mangé, il s'est couché.* — After having eaten, he went to bed. Used when both verbs share the same subject.

# C1 — STRATÉGIQUE
## Persuasion, Nuance, and the Art of Argument

The C1 learner is fluent. What they are developing now is *strategic* French — the language of persuasion, of cultural reference, of irony and understatement. These ten lessons are not grammar lessons; they are lessons in the social and literary use of language as power.

---

## Lesson 1 — La Rhétorique: Persuasion and the French Oratorical Tradition

**Central Insight:** The French oratorical tradition runs from Demosthenes (admired by French classicists), through Cicero (whose Latin every educated Frenchman read until the 20th century), through the Revolutionary speeches of Mirabeau and Danton, to de Gaulle's 1940 BBC broadcast — all built on the same structural logic.

**Cultural Thread:**
De Gaulle's speech of June 18, 1940 — "Quoi qu'il arrive, la flamme de la résistance française ne doit pas s'éteindre et ne s'éteindra pas" — is studied in French schools not just as history but as rhetoric. The conditional (*ne doit pas s'éteindre*) followed by the future indicative (*ne s'éteindra pas*) is a rhetorical escalation: from necessity to certainty. From "must not" to "will not." Every major French political speech has been analyzed in these terms. Rhetoric in France is a subject, a tradition, and a professional skill.

Core rhetoric vocabulary: *l'anaphore* (repetition at sentence start: "Je rêve... Je rêve..."), *l'antithèse* (opposed terms: "Il faut choisir entre vivre libre et mourir esclave"), *la métaphore filée* (extended metaphor), *la litote* (understatement: "ce n'est pas sans mérite"), *l'hyperbole*, *la périphrase*, *la chiasme* (reversal of grammatical structure: "ce n'est pas qu'il manque de volonté, c'est que la volonté lui manque").

Grammar focus: The use of *que* as emphatic structure: *C'est le travail qui paye* → *Ce n'est pas la chance qui détermine le succès, c'est le travail.* The cleft sentence (*c'est... qui/que*) is the most common French rhetorical emphasis device.

---

## Lesson 2 — L'Ironie: French Humor and the Unsaid

**Central Insight:** French irony is not British understatement — it is Voltairean wit: the observation that reduces an absurdity to its logical conclusion and delivers it with a straight face.

**Cultural Thread:** Voltaire's *Candide* (1759) is the irony manual. Dr. Pangloss argues throughout that "this is the best of all possible worlds" while catastrophe follows catastrophe. Voltaire never states his argument — he demonstrates it through absurdity. The French tradition of irony (*l'ironie*, from Greek *eironeia*, feigned ignorance) is performative: it assumes a shared intelligence between speaker and listener, trusts the listener to invert the surface meaning. The *Canard Enchaîné*'s front page operates on this principle.

C1 vocabulary: *sous-entendu* (implied/unstated), *l'ironie, le sarcasme* (heavier than irony), *la dérision* (mockery), *le trait d'esprit* (witty remark), *l'humour noir* (dark humor), *pince-sans-rire* (straight-faced, deadpan — literally "pinch-without-laugh").

Grammar: Indirect speech with temporal shifts; the implicit subject (*On raconte que..., Il paraîtrait que..., Il semblerait que...*) as distancing device.

---

## Lesson 3 — La Nuance Lexicale: Synonyms, Connotation, and Register

**Central Insight:** The difference between *maison, demeure, résidence, logement, pavillon, villa, chaumière* is not semantic — it is connotative, historical, and social.

**Cultural Thread:** *La demeure* (residence, dwelling, from Latin *demorari*, to remain) carries aristocratic weight; *le pavillon* suggests suburban respectability; *le taudis* (slum) carries social stigma; *la chaumière* (thatched cottage) is Romantic literary nostalgia. Every cluster of synonyms in French carries a social register map. The educated French person navigates these maps instinctively — learning them consciously is what C1 requires.

Grammar: The distinction between *savoir* and *connaître* (both = to know, but: *savoir* = know a fact/how to; *connaître* = know a person/place). *Entendre* and *écouter* (both = to hear/listen, but *entendre* = to perceive; *écouter* = active attention). *Voir* and *regarder*, *parler* and *dire* — all the false-synonym pairs that reveal the precision built into French vocabulary.

---

## Lesson 4 — Les Idiomes en Contexte: Idiomatic French and Cultural Literacy

**Central Insight:** Every French idiom is a compressed cultural history. Learning idioms is not learning decoration — it is learning the bones of the culture.

**Cultural Thread:** French idioms frequently involve: animals (*avoir le cafard, poser un lapin, donner sa langue au chat, il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours*), food (*mettre de l'eau dans son vin, c'est la croix et la bannière, casser les pieds*), body parts (*avoir le bras long, tirer les ficelles, faire la fine bouche*), and bureaucracy (*se noyer dans un verre d'eau, avoir le cafard, c'est le serpent qui se mord la queue*). Each has a history — the idiom about the bear's skin comes from a La Fontaine fable (1668); *mettre de l'eau dans son vin* (to moderate one's position) comes from the Roman practice of diluting wine.

Grammar: Idiomatic expressions often violate the grammar rules you've learned — *il faut de tout pour faire un monde* (you need everything to make a world = it takes all sorts) uses an invariable *de tout*; *sauve qui peut!* (every man for himself) uses the subjunctive of *pouvoir* as a command. Recognizing idioms as frozen grammatical forms — not to be parsed — is itself a competence.

---

## Lesson 5 — La Syntaxe Complexe: Ellipsis, Inversion, and Literary Sentence Structure

**Central Insight:** Literary and formal French uses sentence structures that do not appear in spoken French — but that every educated French person reads and recognizes, and that signal mastery.

**Cultural Thread:** The *grand style* of French prose — Flaubert, Proust, Yourcenar — uses syntax the way a composer uses harmony: for emotional and rhythmic effect. Proust's sentences can run for several pages. Yourcenar's *Mémoires d'Hadrien* opens: *"Mon cher Marc, Je me suis décidé à te parler de ma vie."* Simple — but in the Latin epistolary tradition that Yourcenar is consciously invoking.

Grammar focus:
- *Inversion stylistique*: *Jamais il n'avait vu pareille chose.* (He had never seen such a thing.) Fronting the adverb triggers inversion.
- *Ellipsis*: *Lui, travailler ? Jamais.* (Him, work? Never.) Subject-verb ellipsis for dramatic effect.
- *Dislocation*: *Ce roman, je l'ai lu trois fois.* (That novel — I've read it three times.) Left-dislocation for emphasis.
- *L'indéfini on*: In literary French, *on* = one, they, people, the narrator's voice — an elegant way to generalize without specifying.

## Lessons 6–10 — C1 Advanced Content

**Lesson 6 — La Presse Française: Reading French Political Writing**
Core: vocabulary of French political journalism; editorial conventions; how to read a *tribune libre* (opinion piece) vs a *article de fond* (in-depth analysis) vs a *brève* (brief news item). Grammar: The conditional journalistique; the impersonal passive; discourse markers specific to journalistic register.

**Lesson 7 — Les Références Culturelles: Allusions and Intertextuality**
Core: The French cultural canon that educated French people share and reference in conversation — Molière, Voltaire, Victor Hugo, Flaubert, Proust, Camus, Simone de Beauvoir. Being able to recognize and use references (*c'est un Tartuffe* = a hypocrite; *jouer les Hamlet* = to be paralyzed by indecision; *c'est la recherche du temps perdu* = a futile attempt to recover the past). Grammar: Nominalizations derived from proper nouns (*un voltairien, un proustien, avoir le trac*).

**Lesson 8 — Hypothèse et Spéculation: The Conditional Passé and Counterfactuals**
Core: Advanced conditional structures; the subjunctive *imparfait* for literary and formal register (*si j'eusse su...* — if I had known...); speculative language for politics, history, and intellectual discussion. Grammar: The full range of si-clause structures; the conditional of reported speech; epistemic modality in French.

**Lesson 9 — La Négation Nuancée: From Simple to Complex**
Core: The spectrum of French negation — *ne... pas, ne... jamais, ne... rien, ne... personne, ne... plus, ne... que* (only), *ne... guère* (hardly — literary/formal), *ne... point* (archaic but still in formal writing), *ne... nullement* (by no means — formal), *sans que + subjonctif, loin de + infinitif*. Grammar: Double negation strategies; restrictive *ne... que* (not a negation but a restriction); rhetorical litotes built on complex negation.

**Lesson 10 — L'Oral Formel: Conferences, Panels, and Public Speaking**
Core: The vocabulary and structures of formal oral presentation in French; managing interruptions (*si vous me permettez, permettez-moi de terminer*); expressing nuanced agreement (*vous soulevez un point important, toutefois...*); asking clarifying questions in academic/professional settings (*si je vous comprends bien, vous suggérez que...?*). Grammar: Modal verbs in formal register (*il conviendrait de, il y aurait lieu de, on pourrait envisager*); the formal question form with inversion (*à quoi attribuez-vous...?*).

# C2 — MAÎTRISE
## Full Integration — Style, Voice, and the Literature of French

The C2 learner does not study French. They live in it. The ten lessons of C2 are not language instruction — they are invitations into the deepest chambers of the French literary and linguistic tradition. At this level, the teacher steps aside and becomes a guide.

---

## Lesson 1 — Le Français Classique: The Language of Racine and Corneille

**Cultural Thread:** 17th-century classical French (*le français classique*) — the language of Racine's tragedies, Corneille's political theatre, La Fontaine's fables, and Pascal's *Pensées* — is the foundation of modern French style. The *Grand Siècle* (Great Century) under Louis XIV was when French became the language of European diplomacy and culture, replacing Latin. The Académie française (1635) standardized spelling and vocabulary; Vaugelas's *Remarques sur la langue française* (1647) established the principle that good usage follows *le bon usage* — the speech of the educated Parisian court.

Vocabulary: *archaic forms still in use in literature and formal French: quoique (though), nonobstant (notwithstanding), sus (above), supra, ledit/ladite (the aforesaid), icelui/icelle (archaic: he/she), ce faisant (in doing so), partant (consequently — literary)*.

Grammar: *Le subjonctif imparfait* and *plus-que-parfait du subjonctif* (the literary tenses): *Qu'il vînt ou non, la décision était prise.* (Whether he came or not, the decision was made.) — used in formal writing and recognized in all literature; *Le passé simple* as the narrative tense of literature and journalism.

---

## Lesson 2 — La Poésie Française: From Ronsard to Apollinaire

**Cultural Thread:** French poetry has a tradition running from the medieval troubadours (12th century, the first lyric poets of Western Europe, writing in Occitan) through Ronsard's *Sonnets pour Hélène* (1578), Baudelaire's *Les Fleurs du Mal* (1857, tried for obscenity and immortal), Verlaine's *Fêtes galantes*, Rimbaud's *Illuminations*, Apollinaire's *Alcools* (1913, where punctuation was abolished as a compositional decision), Prévert's *Paroles* (1946, the poems that every French schoolchild memorizes). Understanding this lineage — its vocabulary, its versification (the *alexandrin*, the 12-syllable line; the *sonnet*; free verse after Rimbaud) — is understanding the French ear.

Grammar: The *alexandrin* as a rhythm that shaped French syntax — sentences of 12 syllables naturally create a pause (la *césure*) at the sixth syllable. Classical tragedies were written entirely in alexandrins; the rhythm entered prose writing; French sentences often breathe in 12-syllable units without the writer noticing.

---

## Lesson 3 — L'Académie Française: Authority, Controversy, and the Dictionary

**Cultural Thread:** The *Dictionnaire de l'Académie française* is currently in its 9th edition (ongoing since 1986). Its authority is real for the French state (official documents must use Académie-approved vocabulary) and advisory for everyone else (French speakers, including French people, regularly ignore it). The Académie's famous recent battles: against English loanwords (proposing *courriel* for email — ignored; *mèl* — also ignored; the English wins), against gender-inclusive writing (*écriture inclusive* — opposed by the Académie in a 2021 communiqué), and the slow feminization of professional titles (*auteure, professeure, cheffe* — accepted late and reluctantly).

Grammar: *Les rectifications orthographiques de 1990* — the spelling reforms of 1990, still controversial. Key changes: circumflex accent removed from *i* and *u* in most words (*il plaît → il plait; la voûte → la voute*), compound words simplified (*un porte-monnaie → un portemonnaie*), foreign plurals regularized. Many French people still use the old forms; both are accepted in the current exam system.

---

## Lesson 4 — Le Style: Flaubert, Proust, and the Sentence as Architecture

**Cultural Thread:** Flaubert spent days seeking *le mot juste* (the exact word). His principle: in a sentence, each word must be the only possible word in that position. No synonym will do. This pursuit of precision made him write slowly, painfully, and immortally: *Madame Bovary* (1857) is the novel that made the modern novel possible, not because of its story but because of its sentences. Proust, his inheritor, took the principle of precision and applied it to time: his sentences are as long as a memory is complex because the memory's complexity required its own syntactic structure. At C2, reading Flaubert and Proust is not literary tourism — it is language apprenticeship.

Core: Reading exercises in classical French prose; identifying *le mot juste* in a given context; rewriting sentences with greater precision; recognizing Flaubertian irony in description.

---

## Lessons 5–10 — C2 Advanced Content

**Lesson 5 — L'Argot et les Registres Extrêmes: Street French and Its History**
From *le verlan* (back-slang) to Parisian argot (*la caillera, le keuf, le flick*), to the historical argot of 19th-century criminals (*la pègre*) studied by Victor Hugo and Balzac. The etymology of street vocabulary as cultural history.

**Lesson 6 — Les Régionalismes: France's Linguistic Geography**
Breton, Alsatian, Basque, Occitan, Corsican — the minority languages of France and their survival. The 2008 constitutional revision that recognized *les langues régionales* as part of France's heritage, without giving them official status. The ongoing legal and political debate about teaching minority languages in public schools.

**Lesson 7 — La Traduction: What Cannot Cross**
The *intraduisibles* — words that cross no border. *Dépaysement* (the disorienting feeling of being in a foreign country — but not unpleasant). *Saudade* (Portuguese, but discussed in French literary criticism). *Sehnsucht* (German, the same). What these words reveal about the cultures that needed them. And *esprit de corps, je ne sais quoi, raison d'être, coup de grâce, faux pas* — the French words that English absorbed but never digested.

**Lesson 8 — L'Éloquence Formelle: The Art of the French Formal Letter**
The complete system of formal correspondence — from the *formule d'appel* (opening address) to the *formule de politesse* (closing formula). The hierarchy: writing to a minister vs a mayor vs a judge vs a colleague. The formulas that signal mastery: *Veuillez agréer, Monsieur le Président, l'expression de ma haute considération* vs *Je vous adresse, Monsieur, mes salutations distinguées*. Every word in these formulas has a history; knowing why they exist makes them memorable.

**Lesson 9 — Le Double Sens: Ambiguity, Wordplay, and French Humor**
The *contrepèterie* (spoonerism-as-wit — a staple of French adult humor, the subject of entire dictionaries), the *calembour* (pun), the *jeu de mots* (wordplay), the politically satirical wordplay of *Les Guignols de l'Info* (the French political puppet satire show) and *Le Gorafi* (France's satirical news site). French humor at C2 is not just understanding jokes — it is appreciating the linguistic architecture that makes them work.

**Lesson 10 — La Voix: Finding Your Own French**
The final lesson of the entire course. Not a grammar lesson, not a vocabulary lesson. A reflection: what is *votre* French? Which register is natural to you? What is your *accent*? What words have become yours? This lesson is structured as a self-assessment and creative writing exercise — the learner writes a page in French, any topic, any register, and reflects on the choices they made. Because at C2, every choice is intentional. That is what style is.

*End of C2 — Maîtrise*
