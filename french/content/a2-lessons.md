# A2 — Environnement
## Moving Through the World

> *"Voyager, c'est bien utile, ça fait travailler l'imagination."*
> — Louis-Ferdinand Céline, *Voyage au bout de la nuit*

The A1 learner found language in a room. The A2 learner steps outside. These ten lessons build the French needed to *move* — through the day, the city, the market, the doctor's waiting room, the train station. The grammar escalates: past actions enter the picture, the reflexive verb reveals a French self that acts upon itself, and the learner discovers that French has multiple ways of seeing time.

## Lesson 1 — Ma Journée: The Reflexive Self

**Level:** A2 | **Theme:** Daily Routine
**Central Insight:** French reflexive verbs reveal that certain actions are done *to yourself* — French grammar insists on this distinction linguistically in a way English quietly erases.

**Cultural Thread:**
When a French person says *je me lève* (I get myself up), *je me douche* (I shower myself), *je m'habille* (I dress myself), the reflexive pronoun (*me, te, se...*) is grammatically mandatory. This is not an artifice — it is the inheritance of Latin reflexive verbs (*se levare, se lavare*) that marked actions directed back at the agent. French preserved this structure entirely. In doing so, it encoded a philosophy: your daily self-maintenance is a relationship between you and yourself. The body is not an instrument you operate; it is a self you tend. This is, incidentally, compatible with the French emphasis on personal care, *la toilette*, and the entire industry of French cosmetics — the country that invented *se pomponner* (to primp oneself) has grammar that supports the act.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *La Journée — Yourself Through the Day*
"French has a special verb form for things you do to yourself. Getting up, washing, dressing — in French, these actions loop back to the actor. You are both subject and object of your own day."

*Screen 2 — Reflexive Pronouns (concept)*
**me** (myself), **te** (yourself), **se** (himself/herself), **nous** (ourselves), **vous** (yourselves), **se** (themselves)
These pronouns sit between the subject and the verb: *je me lève*, never *je lève me*.
Before a vowel: *m', t', s'* — *je m'habille*.

*Screen 3 — Morning Routine Verbs (vocabulary)*
- **se réveiller** — to wake up (oneself)
- **se lever** — to get up
- **se laver** — to wash (oneself)
- **se doucher** — to shower
- **se brosser les dents** — to brush one's teeth (*les dents* is direct object — French says "the teeth," not "my teeth")
- **s'habiller** — to get dressed
- **se coiffer** — to do one's hair
- **se maquiller** — to put on makeup

*Screen 4 — Evening Routine (vocabulary)*
- **se reposer** — to rest
- **se détendre** — to relax
- **s'endormir** — to fall asleep
- **se coucher** — to go to bed
- **se déshabiller** — to undress

*Screen 5 — Reflexive Verb Conjugation (concept)*
Model: **se lever**
- Je me lève / Tu te lèves / Il/elle se lève
- Nous nous levons / Vous vous levez / Ils/elles se lèvent
Note the accent change: *lever* → *lève* — the *e* becomes *è* under stress. French stress is invisible to the ear (all syllables are more or less equal) but visible on the page through accents. The accent is a pronunciation guide built into the word.

*Screen 6 — The Daily Schedule (examples)*
- **Je me réveille à sept heures.** — I wake up at seven.
- **Elle se douche avant le petit-déjeuner.** — She showers before breakfast.
- **Nous nous couchons tôt le dimanche.** — We go to bed early on Sundays.
- **Il se lève toujours en retard.** — He always gets up late.

*Screen 7 — The French Morning (narrative)*
The French morning is sacred in a specific way: it is slow. The *petit-déjeuner* (literally "little lunch" — the French never called breakfast a "break-fast") is coffee or hot chocolate, a tartine or croissant, and silence or the radio. No one eats eggs and bacon at 7am. The morning is a transition, not a performance. *Prendre le temps de* (to take the time to) is a phrase the French use with real conviction. The morning routine is the daily reassertion of a self before the world intervenes.

*Screen 8 — Time and Frequency Adverbs (vocabulary)*
- **toujours** — always
- **souvent** — often
- **parfois / quelquefois** — sometimes
- **rarement** — rarely
- **jamais** — never (*ne... jamais* in full negation)
- **d'habitude** — usually
- **en ce moment** — right now, these days

*Screen 9 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **D'habitude, je me lève à six heures et demie.** — Usually I get up at half past six.
- **Tu te couches souvent après minuit ?** — Do you often go to bed after midnight?
- **Je ne me maquille jamais le week-end.** — I never put on makeup on weekends.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- Reflexive verbs use *me/te/se/nous/vous/se* before the conjugated verb.
- They describe actions directed at the subject.
- *Se brosser les dents* — body parts take *les*, not possessives, in French.
- Elide reflexive pronouns before vowels: *je m'appelle, elle s'endort*.

**Grammar Point:** Reflexive Verbs — Présent
The reflexive pronoun is part of the verb — it cannot be separated from it in simple tenses. In questions with inversion: *Vous levez-vous tôt ?* — the pronoun stays with the subject even when it's moved.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Je m'appelle* (I call myself) is the first reflexive verb most learners meet, in the sentence *Comment vous appelez-vous ?* The French "what is your name" is "what do you call yourself?" — identity in French is something you assign, something you name yourself into. It is not something given; it is something claimed.

## Lesson 2 — Les Achats: Shopping, Money, and the Marketplace

**Level:** A2 | **Theme:** Shopping & Commerce
**Central Insight:** French shopping vocabulary preserves the tension between the local market and the industrial supermarket — two philosophies of consumption that still coexist in France.

**Cultural Thread:**
France resisted the supermarket longer than most Western European countries. The *marché* (open-air market) — selling local produce, cheese, meat, and fish directly from producer to consumer — remained central to French life through the late 20th century. Many cities and towns still hold weekly markets. The vocabulary reflects this dual world: *le marchand* (the merchant, a word from Middle Ages commerce), *le rayon* (the section/aisle, modern), *le prix* (the price), *marchander* (to bargain — rare now in France but embedded in the vocabulary). France's famous *exception culturelle* extends to commerce: the country has protected local bookshops, small fromageries, and regional producers through law and culture in ways that have no equivalent in the Anglophone world.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Les Achats — The Art of the Exchange*
"Every Saturday morning in French towns, the market happens. The *marché* is not just a place to buy vegetables — it is the last public institution where the price is still sometimes negotiable, the product has a face behind it, and the exchange is a brief social contract."

*Screen 2 — Shopping Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **le marché** — market
- **le supermarché** — supermarket (the rational, efficient antagonist of the marché)
- **la boutique** — shop/boutique (implies specialization and character)
- **la caisse** — till/checkout
- **le rayon** — section (of a supermarket: *le rayon fromages*)
- **le panier** — basket
- **le chariot** — shopping trolley

*Screen 3 — Price Talk (vocabulary)*
- **le prix** — price
- **ça coûte combien ?** — how much does it cost?
- **c'est combien ?** — how much is it?
- **cher / pas cher** — expensive / inexpensive
- **une promotion / une promo** — a sale/deal
- **les soldes** — the sales (seasonal regulated sales in France — twice a year by law)
- **une réduction** — a discount

*Screen 4 — Paying (concept)*
- **payer** — to pay
- **payer en espèces** — to pay cash (*espèces* = species, but in the monetary sense: coined money)
- **payer par carte** — to pay by card
- **faire la monnaie** — to make change
- **le reçu / le ticket de caisse** — receipt
- **rendre la monnaie** — to give back change

*Screen 5 — The Subjective Adjective Pair (compare)*
French commerce has a pair of adjectives with no direct English equivalent:
- **cher** (expensive) / **bon marché** (cheap, literally "good market") — note that *bon marché* never becomes *bonne marchée*; it's invariable, a frozen expression
- **gratuit** (free, as in no cost) — from Latin *gratis*, out of good grace. The distinction between *gratuit* (no charge) and *libre* (free as in freedom) is something French maintains carefully. A *logiciel gratuit* is freeware; a *logiciel libre* is open-source. Cost-free and freedom-free are different things.

*Screen 6 — Quantities (concept)*
- **un kilo de / un kilogramme de** — a kilo of
- **une livre de** — a pound of (*la livre* = 500 grams in French metric convention)
- **un litre de** — a litre of
- **une bouteille de** — a bottle of
- **une boîte de** — a tin/box of
- **un morceau de** — a piece of
- **une tranche de** — a slice of

*Screen 7 — At the Cheese Counter (narrative)*
The French fromagerie (cheese shop) is a study in professional expertise. The *fromager/fromagère* knows their hundreds of varieties — the *appellation d'origine contrôlée* (AOC) system, established in the 1920s for wine and extended to cheese, legally protects regional cheeses from imitation. There are over 1,200 named French cheeses. General de Gaulle famously complained: "How can you govern a country that has 246 varieties of cheese?" The line is better than accurate. At the cheese counter, you ask: *Je peux goûter ?* (May I taste?) — and you always should.

*Screen 8 — Real Exchanges (examples)*
- **Je voudrais deux cents grammes de comté, s'il vous plaît.** — I'd like 200 grams of Comté.
- **C'est combien le kilo ?** — How much per kilo?
- **Vous avez quelque chose de moins cher ?** — Do you have something less expensive?
- **Je vais prendre ça.** — I'll take this.
- **Vous payez comment ?** — How will you pay?

*Screen 9 — Clothing Shopping (vocabulary)*
- **la taille** — size (clothing)
- **la pointure** — shoe size
- **essayer** — to try on
- **la cabine d'essayage** — fitting room
- **ça me va** — it fits me / it suits me
- **trop grand / trop petit** — too big / too small
- **en soldes** — on sale

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- French commerce is dual: the modern supermarket and the traditional specialist shop.
- Quantities use *de/d'* before the noun: *un kilo de fromage*.
- *Cher* = expensive; *bon marché* = cheap (invariable).
- *Les soldes* = the regulated seasonal sales — twice a year, legally controlled.

**Grammar Point:** *De* After Quantity Expressions
After all quantity words (numbers, *un kilo de, une bouteille de, beaucoup de, un peu de, trop de*), the article disappears and *de/d'* is used directly before the noun: *beaucoup de fromage* (not *beaucoup du fromage*). This is consistent — the quantity already specifies how much; the article's job is done.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Les soldes* happen twice a year in France — January and July — regulated by law since 1906. Retailers cannot reduce prices whenever they like; they must wait for the official *soldes* periods. This regulation exists to protect small shops from unfair competition. It means that in France, "sale" has a specific, legal meaning, not the American perpetual-discount meaning. When French people talk about *les soldes*, they mean a culturally significant event, not a permanent marketing strategy.

## Lesson 3 — En Route: Transport and the Art of Going Places

**Level:** A2 | **Theme:** Transport & Travel
**Central Insight:** The French train network (SNCF) is not just infrastructure — it is the nervous system of the Republic, built to connect every corner of the country to Paris.

**Cultural Thread:**
The French railway was built in a star pattern radiating from Paris, reflecting the Jacobin belief that all roads lead to the capital — and from the capital, all decisions flow. Napoleon's road network had done the same a century earlier. To understand French transport is to understand French centralization: you cannot go from Lyon to Bordeaux by train without changing in Paris or using a regional line so slow it is a statement. The SNCF (*Société Nationale des Chemins de fer Français*), founded in 1938, is one of Europe's great symbols of state infrastructure — a company that is also an institution, a union stronghold, and a cultural symbol. French strikes frequently start on the railways.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *En Route — The Republic in Motion*
"The French state built roads and railways from Paris outward, like rays of sun from a capital that believed itself the center of civilization. To travel in France is to understand this centralization physically."

*Screen 2 — Transport Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **le métro** — metro/subway (Paris Métro, opened 1900)
- **le bus** / **l'autobus** — bus
- **le tram** / **le tramway** — tram
- **le train** — train
- **le TGV** — *Train à Grande Vitesse* (high-speed train, introduced 1981)
- **l'avion** — airplane
- **la voiture** — car
- **le vélo** / **le vélib'** — bicycle / Paris bike-share
- **à pied** — on foot

*Screen 3 — At the Station (vocabulary)*
- **la gare** — train station (from Germanic *wara*, guard, protection — a place you are kept safe while waiting)
- **le quai** — platform
- **le billet** — ticket (*billet* originally meant small letter, note — a ticket as a written promise)
- **aller simple** — one-way ticket
- **aller-retour** — return ticket
- **composter** — to validate (stamp) your ticket — mandatory in France before boarding older trains; the *composteur* machine is orange and at every platform entrance

*Screen 4 — Directions (concept)*
- **Où est... ?** — Where is...?
- **Comment aller à... ?** — How do I get to...?
- **Prenez la ligne + [number].** — Take line [number].
- **Changez à... ** — Change at...
- **Descendez à... ** — Get off at...
- **C'est à... arrêts.** — It's ... stops away.
- **En face de** — opposite, facing
- **Au coin de** — at the corner of

*Screen 5 — Buying a Ticket (examples)*
- **Un aller simple pour Lyon, s'il vous plaît.** — A one-way to Lyon, please.
- **À quelle heure part le prochain train ?** — What time does the next train leave?
- **Il y a un retard ?** — Is there a delay?
- **Le train est supprimé.** — The train is cancelled. (A phrase French travelers know too well.)
- **N'oubliez pas de composter votre billet.** — Don't forget to validate your ticket.

*Screen 6 — SNCF Culture (narrative)*
The French railway strike (*la grève*) is both a disruption and a constitutional tradition. The right to strike is embedded in the French Constitution of 1946 — it is not merely tolerated, it is legally protected. SNCF workers have among the strongest union protections in France. A strike at the SNCF is usually preceded by *un préavis de grève* (strike notice) five days in advance, which allows travelers to change plans. This forewarning is itself legislated. The French have institutionalized the right to disrupt the state in a way that is peculiarly French: organized, announced, and periodically necessary.

*Screen 7 — The Car and Its Codes (narrative)*
France drives on the right. Speed limits: 50 km/h in towns, 80 km/h on regional roads, 110 on non-motorway dual carriageways, 130 on motorways (*autoroutes*). The motorway network is mostly tolled (*péage*). The *priorité à droite* rule (priority to the right at unmarked intersections) still applies in many areas and has caused more accidents than any other French traffic rule — foreigners, and even some French drivers, regularly forget it.

*Screen 8 — Asking for Help (examples)*
- **Excusez-moi, je cherche la station de métro la plus proche.** — Excuse me, I'm looking for the nearest metro station.
- **Est-ce qu'il y a un bus pour aller au centre-ville ?** — Is there a bus to go downtown?
- **C'est loin à pied ?** — Is it far on foot?
- **Je me suis perdu(e).** — I got lost. (reflexive again — you lost yourself)

*Screen 9 — The Paris Metro (cultural slide)*
The Paris Métro has 16 lines and 302 stations — one of the densest subway networks in the world. Its stations were originally designed by Hector Guimard in Art Nouveau style (1900); the original green iron entrances with their organic curves are protected monuments. The Métro has its own smell, its own acoustics, its own poets. Francis Ponge, the poet of objects, once described the Paris metro as "a subterranean republic." Line 1 runs through the historical axis of Paris (from Vincennes to La Défense), passing the Louvre, the Champs-Élysées, and Place de la Concorde. To ride Line 1 is to ride through French history.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- French transport vocabulary: *le train, le métro, le bus, la voiture, l'avion, à pied*.
- Validate your ticket (*composter*) before boarding older trains — mandatory.
- *Aller simple* (one-way) / *aller-retour* (return).
- SNCF strikes are culturally and constitutionally embedded.
- Paris Métro: 16 lines, Art Nouveau entrances, a subterranean republic.

**Grammar Point:** The Imperative (Commands)
*Prenez la ligne 4. Descendez à Châtelet. Tournez à gauche.*
The imperative uses: *tu* form (drop the *-s* for -*er* verbs: *parle!* not *parles!*), *vous* form (same as indicative), *nous* form (= "let's": *allons!*).
Giving directions requires the imperative constantly. It is one of the first grammatically sophisticated things learners can do.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Composter son billet* — validating/stamping your ticket — is an obligation on many older French trains that confuses visitors. The French railway honor system works like this: you buy a ticket and must stamp it yourself before boarding. Inspectors come through the train; if you have an unstamped ticket, you pay a fine, regardless of having paid. The machine is always at the platform entrance, orange or yellow, the size of a desktop printer. The sound it makes — a sharp mechanical clunk — is as French as the Eiffel Tower to regular train travelers.

## Lesson 4 — La Santé: Health, the Body in Time

**Level:** A2 | **Theme:** Health & Wellbeing
**Central Insight:** France has one of the world's most sophisticated public health systems — and a cultural relationship with illness, medicine, and hypochondria that is uniquely French.

**Cultural Thread:**
France's *Sécurité Sociale* (*la Sécu*) was founded in 1945 by Ambroise Croizat — a communist minister in De Gaulle's provisional government — who had the vision to create a system where no one would pay for illness. It covers 70-100% of most medical expenses. The French go to the doctor more often than almost any other nationality. They consume more prescribed medication per capita than any other country in Europe. The philosopher Michel Foucault, in *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963), argued that modern medicine invented a particular "medical gaze" — a way of seeing the body as a territory of disease to be mapped and controlled. French culture has this gaze deeply embedded. The *médecin traitant* (registered GP) is not optional; you must have one registered to receive full reimbursement. The body is bureaucratically administered.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *La Santé — The Medicalized Republic*
"France has a word for the right to health: it is in the preamble of the 1946 Constitution. 'The Nation guarantees to all the protection of health.' French medicine is not a service — it is a constitutional right."

*Screen 2 — Health Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **la santé** — health (*à votre santé!* — to your health! — the French toast)
- **malade** — sick/ill (from Latin *male habitus* — badly held)
- **la maladie** — illness
- **guérir** — to heal/recover
- **soigner** — to treat/care for
- **le médecin** — doctor (*médecin traitant* = registered GP)
- **l'infirmier/ère** — nurse
- **l'urgence** — emergency (*les urgences* = A&E)

*Screen 3 — Symptoms (vocabulary)*
- **avoir de la fièvre** — to have a fever
- **tousser** — to cough
- **éternuer** — to sneeze
- **avoir mal à la gorge** — to have a sore throat
- **se sentir fatigué(e)** — to feel tired
- **avoir des nausées** — to feel nauseous
- **vomir** — to vomit (clinical, but commonly used)
- **avoir le vertige** — to feel dizzy

*Screen 4 — At the Doctor's: The Exchange (examples)*
- **Qu'est-ce qui vous amène ?** — What brings you in? (the doctor's opening question)
- **J'ai mal ici depuis deux jours.** — I've had pain here for two days.
- **Depuis quand est-ce que vous toussez ?** — How long have you been coughing?
- **Vous avez des antécédents ?** — Any medical history?
- **Je vais vous examiner.** — I'm going to examine you.
- **Je vais vous faire une ordonnance.** — I'm going to write you a prescription.

*Screen 5 — The Pharmacy (concept)*
**Obligatory at French pharmacies:**
- Green illuminated cross = open pharmacy
- *Médicaments sur ordonnance* — prescription medication
- *Médicaments sans ordonnance* — over-the-counter medication (*sans ordonnance*)
- *Remboursé / Non remboursé* — reimbursed by Sécu / not reimbursed
The pharmacist can advise on *médicaments sans ordonnance* and minor ailments. This is expected and normal in French culture.

*Screen 6 — French Medication Culture (narrative)*
The French have a particular love of suppositories (*les suppositoires*) — one of the most culturally distinctive facts of French medicine. Medications often come in a suppository form that would be prescribed as a pill elsewhere. This is not a joke; it reflects a French medical tradition that believes the rectal route is often more effective for rapid absorption. Foreign visitors to France are occasionally surprised by prescriptions. This is noted not for humor but because it is genuinely part of navigating French healthcare as a foreigner.

*Screen 7 — Emergency French (concept)*
- **Appelez le SAMU !** — Call the emergency medical service! (15 = SAMU, the French emergency ambulance service)
- **J'ai besoin d'aide.** — I need help.
- **C'est urgent.** — It's urgent.
- **Il y a eu un accident.** — There has been an accident.
- **Quelqu'un est blessé.** — Someone is injured.
French emergency numbers: **15** (SAMU — medical), **17** (Police), **18** (Pompiers — fire/rescue), **112** (European universal emergency).

*Screen 8 — Insurance and Administration (concept)*
- **la Sécurité sociale** (*la Sécu*) — the French national health system
- **la mutuelle** — complementary private health insurance (covers what Sécu doesn't)
- **la carte Vitale** — the French health insurance card (green, credit-card-sized, contains your Social Security number)
- **le ticket modérateur** — the portion you pay
- **remboursement** — reimbursement

*Screen 9 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Je n'ai pas ma carte Vitale avec moi.** — I don't have my Carte Vitale with me.
- **Est-ce que vous avez une mutuelle ?** — Do you have complementary insurance?
- **Ce médicament est remboursé à 65%.** — This medication is 65% covered.
- **Vous pouvez me donner un arrêt de travail ?** — Can you give me a sick note?

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- French health system: universal coverage, *médecin traitant* required, *carte Vitale* for access.
- Pharmacy is a medical first stop; *sans ordonnance* advice is normal.
- Emergency: 15 (SAMU), 17 (Police), 18 (Pompiers), 112 (EU general).
- French hypochondria is culturally sanctioned and medically supported.

**Grammar Point:** *Depuis* + Present Tense
To describe something that started in the past and continues now:
*J'ai mal à la tête depuis ce matin.* — I've had a headache since this morning.
French uses the **present tense** with *depuis* — not the past tense. English says "I have had"; French says "I have." The present tense in French is more expansive — it includes continuations from the past.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Comment vous sentez-vous ?* (How do you feel?) is the formal register. The informal is *comment tu te sens ?*. Both are normal. But French people are not particularly effusive about health complaints in social settings. Unlike some cultures where health problems are discussed openly in conversation, the French tend to keep detailed medical complaints private, or in a consultation room. *Ça va* suffices socially.

## Lesson 5 — La Météo: Weather, the Seasons, and Why the French Complain

**Level:** A2 | **Theme:** Weather
**Central Insight:** The French relationship with weather is a conversation, not a report.

**Cultural Thread:**
Weather is the universal social lubricant, but in France it has a specific function: it is the one topic on which French people will speak to strangers. The French reputation for coldness toward strangers partly dissolves in front of bad weather. *Il fait un temps de chien* (it's dog weather — terrible), *il fait un froid de canard* (duck cold — freezing), *il y a du brouillard à couper au couteau* (fog you could cut with a knife) — weather idioms in French are richly metaphorical because complaining about weather is a genuine art form. The French *grommeler* (to grumble) is not pessimism; it is a communal activity, an assertion of discernment, a way of being together in shared suffering. Weather bonds people who would otherwise have nothing to say to each other.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *La Météo — National Conversation Topic Number One*
"In France, complaining about the weather is not small talk. It is art. The vocabulary of French weather includes expressions so vivid they make the thermometer irrelevant."

*Screen 2 — Basic Weather (vocabulary)*
- **Il fait beau.** — The weather is nice. (*faire* is used for weather — literally "it makes beautiful")
- **Il fait mauvais.** — The weather is bad.
- **Il fait chaud / froid.** — It's hot / cold.
- **Il pleut.** — It's raining. (*pleuvoir* — to rain, irregular)
- **Il neige.** — It's snowing. (*neiger*)
- **Il y a du vent.** — It's windy.
- **Il y a du brouillard.** — It's foggy.
- **Il y a du soleil.** — It's sunny.

*Screen 3 — Temperature (concept)*
*Quelle température fait-il ?* — What's the temperature?
*Il fait + [number] + degrés.* — It's [number] degrees.
France uses Celsius. The freezing point is *zéro degré* (0°C). A hot summer day is *trente-cinq degrés* (35°C). The famous Paris canicule (heatwave) of August 2003 reached 40°C and killed approximately 15,000 people — mostly elderly, in a city without air conditioning because air conditioning had not been considered necessary. The climate vocabulary has since gained *canicule* (heatwave) as an emergency term.

*Screen 4 — Seasons and Weather Patterns (narrative)*
- **Printemps** — Spring: *les giboulées de mars* (April showers — literally March showers in French, because the French notice weather earlier)
- **Été** — Summer: the *canicule* risk; the *exodus* (the annual August holiday migration when 30 million French leave for vacations simultaneously)
- **Automne** — Autumn: *les feuilles mortes* (dead leaves — Prévert's poem; the song by Yves Montand)
- **Hiver** — Winter: rarely severe in most of France; the exception is the mountains (Alps, Pyrénées) and the northeast

*Screen 5 — Weather Idioms (vocabulary)*
- **un temps de chien** — terrible weather (dog weather)
- **un froid de canard** — biting cold (duck cold — ducks' hunting season is cold)
- **un soleil de plomb** — lead sun — oppressive, flat summer heat
- **sous la pluie battante** — in driving rain
- **une chaleur étouffante** — stifling heat
- **un ciel de traîne** — a sky with trailing clouds after a front

*Screen 6 — Weather Forecasting Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **la météo** — weather forecast
- **les prévisions** — the forecast/predictions
- **les nuages** — clouds
- **les averses** — showers (scattered rain)
- **un orage** — a thunderstorm
- **la foudre** — lightning (literally: the thunderbolt)
- **le tonnerre** — thunder
- **l'arc-en-ciel** — rainbow (literally: the arc in the sky)

*Screen 7 — Giving Opinions on Weather (examples)*
- **Quel temps affreux !** — What awful weather!
- **Il fait un temps magnifique aujourd'hui.** — The weather is magnificent today.
- **J'espère qu'il ne va pas pleuvoir demain.** — I hope it won't rain tomorrow.
- **Selon la météo, il fera beau ce week-end.** — According to the forecast, it'll be nice this weekend.
- **Je déteste la chaleur.** — I hate the heat.

*Screen 8 — Climate Vocabulary (concept)*
**Le changement climatique** (climate change) has entered everyday vocabulary:
- **les canicules** are more frequent
- **les inondations** (floods) are regular in the Loire valley and the Rhône
- **la sécheresse** (drought) is now an annual concern in southern France
- **la tempête** (storm) — France was devastated by the storms of 1999 (*la tempête du siècle*)

*Screen 9 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Il paraît qu'il va neiger cette semaine.** — Apparently it's going to snow this week.
- **Avec ce temps, je préfère rester à la maison.** — In this weather I'd rather stay home.
- **Prends ton imperméable — il risque de pleuvoir.** — Take your raincoat — it might rain.
- **Le brouillard m'empêche de conduire.** — The fog is preventing me from driving.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- *Il fait* + adjective or noun for most weather conditions.
- *Il pleut, il neige* — specific verbs for rain and snow.
- *Il y a* + noun for wind, fog, sun.
- Weather idioms are rich and worth learning — they sound like a native.
- Celsius: 0° freezing, 20° pleasant, 35°+ canicule.

**Grammar Point:** The Near Future — *Futur Proche* vs *Futur Simple*
*Il va pleuvoir* (it's going to rain — near future, spoken) vs *il pleuvra* (it will rain — future simple, more formal/written).
Weather forecasts use both. *Futur proche* for today; *futur simple* for the week ahead (in formal forecasts). The emotional difference: *je vais partir* (I'm about to leave, soon) vs *je partirai* (I will leave, commitment, more distant).

**Teacher's Note:**
*Les giboulées de mars* — March showers — are in French a cultural institution. The March sky that delivers hail, sunshine, rain, and wind within an hour is so reliably unpredictable that it has its own compound word. Prévert's 1945 poem *Les Feuilles Mortes* (Dead Leaves/Autumn Leaves) gave French autumn its soundtrack — "les feuilles mortes se ramassent à la pelle" — autumn leaves are swept up with a shovel. The song made the color of autumn inseparable from regret in French.

## Lesson 6 — À L'École: Education and the Republican School

**Level:** A2 | **Theme:** Education & School
**Central Insight:** The French school (*l'école républicaine*) is not just an educational institution — it is the machine through which France has tried, for 150 years, to create its citizens.

**Cultural Thread:**
Jules Ferry's education reforms of 1881-1882 created the French public school as it exists today: free, compulsory, and — crucially — secular (*laïque*). Ferry's vision was explicitly political: the school would replace the Church as the moral and intellectual formation of French children. The schoolteacher (*l'instituteur/institutrice*) became, in rural France, as important as the priest — sometimes more so. The French baccalauréat (*le bac*), the examination at the end of secondary school, is a national institution — on its results day, France holds its breath. The *grande école* system (Sciences Po, Polytechnique, HEC) creates the elite that runs France. The school is where France makes the French.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *L'École — La République en Miniature*
"Every French child goes through the same school system: the same subjects, the same textbooks, the same exam. The school is how the Republic reproduces itself — one generation at a time."

*Screen 2 — School Levels (vocabulary)*
- **la maternelle** — nursery/preschool (ages 3-6, optional but 97% attendance — the French state care for children starts young)
- **l'école primaire** — primary school (ages 6-11)
- **le collège** — middle school (ages 11-15) — *attention: not a college*
- **le lycée** — high school (ages 15-18)
- **l'université** — university
- **les grandes écoles** — elite professional schools (competitive entry post-bac)

*Screen 3 — School Subjects (vocabulary)*
- **le français** — French language/literature
- **les mathématiques** / **les maths** — mathematics
- **l'histoire-géographie** — history-geography (always paired in French curriculum)
- **les sciences** — sciences (SVT = Life and Earth Sciences; Physics-Chemistry)
- **les langues vivantes** — living languages (foreign languages)
- **la philosophie** — philosophy (compulsory in *terminale*, the final year of lycée — unique in the world)
- **l'éducation physique** — PE
- **les arts plastiques** — visual arts

*Screen 4 — The Bac (concept)*
**Le baccalauréat** — the exam that ends secondary school and opens the door to university.
Originally created by Napoleon in 1808 as an examination for a *literati* class. Now taken by 80% of school-leavers. The *bac philo* — the philosophy paper on the first day — is a national event. This year's questions are debated on the radio, analyzed in newspapers, and argued at dinner tables. Questions like: *"La conscience est-elle un obstacle à l'action ?"* (Is consciousness an obstacle to action?) or *"Peut-on avoir raison contre les autres ?"* (Can one be right against others?).

*Screen 5 — Study Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **apprendre** — to learn
- **étudier** — to study
- **comprendre** — to understand
- **réviser** — to revise/review
- **passer un examen** — to take an exam (not *faire* — you *pass* through an exam in French)
- **réussir** — to succeed/pass
- **échouer / rater** — to fail
- **une note** — a grade (French grading: 0-20, where 10 is passing and 16+ is excellent)

*Screen 6 — The French Grading System (concept)*
France grades on a scale of **0 to 20**. 10/20 is the passing grade. 20/20 is almost never given — it would imply perfection, which French teachers consider impossible. 14/20 is a good grade. 16-17/20 is excellent. 18+/20 is legendary. The French system is designed to discriminate at the top, not to congratulate — which is culturally consistent: praise in French intellectual tradition is earned by rarity, not distributed by encouragement.

*Screen 7 — Classroom Language (examples)*
- **Je n'ai pas compris.** — I didn't understand.
- **Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ?** — Could you repeat that, please?
- **Comment dit-on... en français ?** — How do you say... in French?
- **Qu'est-ce que ça veut dire ?** — What does that mean?
- **J'ai une question.** — I have a question.
- **Pouvez-vous parler plus lentement ?** — Could you speak more slowly?

*Screen 8 — University (concept)*
French university is almost free: around €170/year for a licence (bachelor's degree). The *grandes écoles* are selective and free (or subsidized). French students pay almost nothing for higher education — this is a deliberate Republican principle: knowledge should not be for sale. The consequence is a massified university system with under-resourced campuses and an elite *grandes écoles* system that produces 80% of France's senior leaders despite educating 5% of students.

*Screen 9 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Il est en première au lycée Victor Hugo.** — He's in year 12 (first year of final cycle) at Victor Hugo High School.
- **Elle prépare le bac cette année.** — She's preparing for the bac this year.
- **J'ai eu 14 sur 20 en maths.** — I got 14 out of 20 in maths.
- **Il faut réviser pour l'examen de jeudi.** — You need to revise for Thursday's exam.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- French school system: maternelle → primaire → collège → lycée → université/grandes écoles.
- *Le bac* ends lycée and opens university. Philosophy paper on day one.
- Grades: 0-20. 10 = passing. 20 = theoretical, never achieved.
- Education in France is a constitutional right and almost entirely free.
- Philosophy is compulsory for all French school-leavers.

**Grammar Point:** *Passer*, *Réussir*, *Rater* — The False Friends
*Passer un examen* = to sit/take an exam (not to pass it). *Réussir un examen* = to pass an exam. *Rater/échouer à un examen* = to fail. This is a major false friend: English "pass an exam" ≠ French "passer un examen." The French verb means to go through it, to traverse it. The result is separate.

**Teacher's Note:**
The philosophy exam (*le bac philo*) on the first morning of the baccalauréat lasts four hours. Students choose one of three essay questions and write a structured argumentation — thesis, antithesis, synthesis — entirely from their own reasoning and the philosophical concepts they've studied. There is no multiple choice. There are no notes permitted. The essay is the only assessment. This is the French belief that education forms judgment, not just knowledge. And it produces 700,000 eighteen-year-olds who have written a philosophy essay.

## Lesson 7 — Au Travail: Work, Profession, and the French Relationship with Labor

**Level:** A2 | **Theme:** Work & Employment
**Central Insight:** France has a legally protected 35-hour working week, five weeks of mandatory paid vacation, and a culture that considers overwork a moral failure.

**Cultural Thread:**
The *loi Aubry* (Aubry law) of 2000 instituted the 35-hour working week as a legal maximum. The aim was to distribute employment more broadly. The result was a cultural shift: *le droit au repos* (the right to rest) was formalized. The French concept of *l'art de vivre* (the art of living) — which includes long lunches, summer holidays, and the categorical refusal to work on Sunday in most sectors — is not laziness. It is a political philosophy: work is means, not end. The human being is not an instrument of production. French labor law is among the most protective in the world; French productivity per working hour is among the highest.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Le Travail — A Means, Not an End*
"In France, the working week is legally limited to 35 hours. You have five weeks of paid vacation guaranteed by law. The philosophy: you are not your job."

*Screen 2 — Professions (vocabulary)*
- **le médecin** — doctor
- **l'enseignant(e)** — teacher
- **l'ingénieur(e)** — engineer
- **le/la juriste** — lawyer/legal professional
- **l'infirmier/infirmière** — nurse
- **le/la commercial(e)** — sales representative
- **l'artisan** — craftsperson, artisan
- **le fonctionnaire** — civil servant (a significant portion of France's working population)

*Screen 3 — Talking About Work (concept)*
Formula: **Qu'est-ce que vous faites dans la vie ?** — What do you do for a living? (literally: what do you do in life?)
Answer: **Je suis + [profession]** — no article before profession after *être*
- *Je suis médecin.* NOT *Je suis un médecin.*
Exception: with an adjective — *Je suis un excellent médecin* — the article returns when the profession is qualified.

*Screen 4 — Workplace Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **le bureau** — office/desk
- **l'entreprise** — company
- **la société** — company (more formal)
- **le poste** — position/job
- **le collègue** — colleague
- **le/la chef(fe)** — boss (*chef* is also the word for chef in cooking — the one in charge)
- **la réunion** — meeting
- **l'horaire** — schedule/timetable

*Screen 5 — The French Workplace Culture (narrative)*
The French workplace has a strict hierarchy (*la hiérarchie*) that is simultaneously formal and human. You use *vous* with your manager until they offer *tu*. You shake hands with every colleague when you arrive and when you leave. The *pot* (the end-of-meeting or end-of-week drinks in the office) is a social institution. The lunch break is one to two hours — the French government strongly discourages the desk lunch. The *comité d'entreprise* (works council) gives employees formal legal rights over workplace conditions.

*Screen 6 — Work Expressions (vocabulary)*
- **travailler à plein temps** — to work full-time
- **travailler à mi-temps** — to work part-time (half-time)
- **être en congé** — to be on leave
- **les congés payés** — paid leave (five weeks minimum by law — a 1936 social conquest)
- **le chômage** — unemployment
- **être au chômage** — to be unemployed
- **chercher un emploi** — to look for work

*Screen 7 — The 1936 Conquests (cultural slide)*
The phrase *les congés payés* (paid leave) is used with historical weight in France. Paid vacation was won by the labor movement in 1936, during the *Front Populaire* (Popular Front) government of Léon Blum. For millions of French workers, it was the first time in their lives they had both money and time simultaneously. The photographs of French workers on their first paid vacation — at the seaside, on trains, in fields — became iconic. The right to rest is not an administrative rule in France. It is a social victory with photographs.

*Screen 8 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Je travaille dans une PME depuis trois ans.** — I've worked at an SME for three years.
- **Elle cherche un poste à temps plein.** — She's looking for a full-time position.
- **J'ai une réunion à neuf heures.** — I have a meeting at nine.
- **Nous avons cinq semaines de congés payés par an.** — We have five weeks of paid leave per year.
- **Il est en arrêt maladie depuis une semaine.** — He's been on sick leave for a week.

*Screen 9 — Job Seeking (concept)*
- **le CV** (*curriculum vitae*) — résumé (standard format; French CV includes a photo, which is normal and legal)
- **la lettre de motivation** — cover letter (important in France; formal structure expected)
- **un entretien d'embauche** — a job interview
- **postuler** — to apply
- **être embauché(e)** — to be hired
- **toucher un salaire** — to receive a salary (*toucher* = to touch/receive — money is physically "touched")

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- French professions after *être*: no article.
- 35-hour week + 5 weeks paid leave = legal minimum.
- *Les congés payés* — a 1936 social conquest, treated with historical respect.
- French workplace: formal hierarchy, handshakes on arrival/departure, *vous* with management.
- The French work to live; they do not live to work.

**Grammar Point:** *Depuis* vs *Pendant* vs *Pour*
- *Depuis trois ans* — for three years (ongoing, started in past, continues)
- *Pendant trois ans* — for three years (completed action in the past)
- *Pour trois ans* — for three years (intended future duration)
These three are the temporal precision of French — the language forces you to specify whether time is ongoing, completed, or planned.

**Teacher's Note:**
The French CV includes a photograph. This is normal, legal, and standard. The French *lettre de motivation* is a highly formalized document: it begins formally, states why you are writing, what you offer, and closes with a specific formula (*Veuillez agréer, Monsieur/Madame, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées* — or a modern variant). Writing a French cover letter incorrectly signals, to a French HR professional, that you don't understand French professional culture. This is covered in the B2 secret lesson.

## Lesson 8 — La Ville: City, Neighbourhood, and Urban France

**Level:** A2 | **Theme:** City & Directions
**Central Insight:** French cities are built around public space — the *place publique*, the *rue piétonne*, the *jardin public* — because French republicanism believed that democracy happens in shared physical space.

**Cultural Thread:**
Haussmann's renovation of Paris (1853-1870), ordered by Napoleon III, did not just widen streets to prevent barricades (though it did that too). It created a city designed around the *boulevard* — wide, tree-lined, flanked by uniform buildings with regulated height and material. The *café de la presse*, the *brasserie*, the *tabac* — all are urban institutions designed for the public street. To be *en ville* (in town) in France is to participate in public life. The *flâneur* — the urban wanderer, theorized by Baudelaire and analyzed by Walter Benjamin — is a specifically French-Parisian figure: someone who walks the city as a form of thought.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *La Ville — The Republic Made Physical*
"Haussmann built Paris not just for beauty, but for movement. The boulevard replaced the medieval alley. Wide streets for parades, for markets, for the press of citizens. The French city is the Republic in stone."

*Screen 2 — City Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **le centre-ville** — city center
- **le quartier** — neighbourhood/district
- **l'arrondissement** — Paris district (Paris has 20; other cities may have arrondissements)
- **la banlieue** — suburb (often misunderstood: in French, *la banlieue* can mean both affluent suburbs and the stigmatized outer ring of social housing)
- **la zone piétonne** — pedestrian zone
- **la place** — public square
- **le boulevard** — boulevard

*Screen 3 — Shops and Services (vocabulary)*
- **la boulangerie** — bakery
- **la pharmacie** — pharmacy
- **la poste** — post office
- **la mairie** — town hall (*la mairie* is essential in France — marriages, registrations, and many administrative acts happen there)
- **la bibliothèque** — library
- **le commissariat** — police station
- **la banque** — bank
- **le syndicat d'initiative / l'office de tourisme** — tourist office

*Screen 4 — Asking Directions (concept)*
- **Excusez-moi, où est... ?** — Excuse me, where is...?
- **C'est par où, le musée ?** — Which way is the museum?
- **Vous pouvez m'indiquer... ?** — Could you show me...?
- **Tournez à gauche / à droite.** — Turn left / right.
- **Continuez tout droit.** — Continue straight on.
- **C'est au bout de la rue.** — It's at the end of the street.
- **C'est en face de...** — It's opposite...
- **C'est à dix minutes à pied.** — It's ten minutes on foot.

*Screen 5 — Urban Landmarks (vocabulary)*
- **la cathédrale** — cathedral
- **le château** — castle/palace
- **le musée** — museum
- **le jardin public** — public garden/park
- **le marché** — market
- **la fontaine** — fountain
- **le monument** — monument
- **le pont** — bridge

*Screen 6 — Arrondissements of Paris (narrative)*
Paris's 20 arrondissements spiral outward from the center like a snail shell (the *escargot* plan, set in 1860). The first (*1er*) is the Louvre, the second the press quarter, the third and fourth the Marais, the fifth the Latin Quarter, the sixth Saint-Germain-des-Prés... Each arrondissement has a character, a social register, a price of coffee. *Habiter dans le 16e* (living in the 16th) signals conservative wealth; *habiter dans le 20e* signals bohemian tradition; *habiter dans le 18e* signals the Montmartre mix of tourism and neighborhood life. The arrondissement is a social address.

*Screen 7 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **La mairie est à deux rues d'ici, à droite.** — The town hall is two streets from here, on the right.
- **Prenez la première rue à gauche après le feu.** — Take the first street on the left after the traffic light.
- **Le quartier du Marais est très animé le week-end.** — The Marais neighbourhood is very lively at weekends.
- **Je cherche la rue de Rivoli — c'est loin ?** — I'm looking for Rue de Rivoli — is it far?

*Screen 8 — The French Tabac (cultural slide)*
*Le tabac* — the tobacconist — is one of France's most beloved urban institutions. Beyond selling cigarettes (French tobacco tax revenue funds part of the social system), the *tabac* sells lottery tickets (*le loto*), *timbres fiscaux* (administrative stamps needed for paperwork), transport tickets, and newspapers. Many have a *PMU* (horse racing betting counter) and a zinc bar. The *tabac* is the one-stop administrative-social institution of the French street. Nearly every French person knows their local *tabac* by name.

*Screen 9 — Prepositions in the City (concept)*
Movement verbs take *à* for destinations: *aller à la mairie, aller au marché, aller aux urgences*.
But: countries and regions use *en* or *au/aux*: *aller en France, aller au Maroc, aller aux États-Unis*.
Feminine countries → *en*: *en France, en Espagne*.
Masculine countries → *au*: *au Canada, au Portugal*.
Plural countries → *aux*: *aux États-Unis*.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- City vocabulary: *le quartier, l'arrondissement, la mairie, la boulangerie...*
- Directions: imperative forms (*tournez, continuez, prenez*).
- Paris arrondissements spiral from 1 to 20 — each a social address.
- *Le tabac* is an administrative-social-cultural institution unique to France.

**Grammar Point:** The Imperative for Giving Directions
*Tournez (vous) / tourne (tu)* — turn
*Allez tout droit / va tout droit* — go straight
*Prenez la deuxième rue à gauche / prends...* — take the second street on the left
The imperative *-er* verb drops the *s* in the *tu* form: *tourne*, not *tournes*. But when followed by a pronoun: *vas-y!* (go on!) — the *s* returns for euphony. The mouth's needs override the rule.

**Teacher's Note:**
The French give directions in a specific sequence: they orient first (*vous êtes là*), then give the instruction (*tournez à gauche*), then confirm a landmark (*vous verrez la mairie*). The landmark confirmation is important because French city streets often change name at each block — sometimes four times in a kilometre. The landmark is the reliable anchor.

## Lesson 9 — Les Loisirs: Leisure and the Sacred Weekend

**Level:** A2 | **Theme:** Leisure & Hobbies
**Central Insight:** The French concept of *les loisirs* (leisure activities) is not mere entertainment — it is the domain of freely chosen selfhood, protected by the same Republican tradition that protects labor rights.

**Cultural Thread:**
The sociologist Joffre Dumazedier coined the concept of *civilisation du loisir* (civilization of leisure) in 1962, arguing that the progressive reduction of working hours was creating, for the first time in history, a society where the majority had significant free time. France became the test case for his theory. The *35 heures*, the *congés payés*, the Sunday rest — together they created a culture where leisure is not a reward for work but a right alongside it. The French spend a high proportion of their income on culture, sport, and food. The *association* (voluntary association) is the backbone of French civil society: from the local pétanque club to the municipal choir to the political party.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Les Loisirs — The Art of the Free Hour*
"France has legislated leisure into existence. When you have five weeks of paid vacation and a 35-hour week, free time becomes a cultural institution. What you do with it reveals who you are."

*Screen 2 — Leisure Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **les loisirs** — leisure activities
- **le sport** — sport
- **la lecture** — reading
- **la cuisine** — cooking
- **le cinéma** — cinema
- **le théâtre** — theatre
- **la randonnée** — hiking
- **la pêche** — fishing
- **le jardinage** — gardening
- **la peinture** — painting

*Screen 3 — Sports Vocabulary (vocabulary)*
- **faire du football** / **jouer au football** — to play football (*faire du* = practise the sport; *jouer à* = play the game)
- **faire de la natation** — to swim
- **faire du vélo** — to cycle
- **faire de la randonnée** — to hike
- **jouer au tennis** — to play tennis
- **faire de la musculation** — to do weights/gym
- **pratiquer un sport** — to practise a sport

*Screen 4 — Faire du vs Jouer à (concept)*
Two verb constructions for leisure:
**faire + du/de la/de l'/des** — practise/do (individual sports, activities)
*Je fais de la natation. Elle fait du yoga. Nous faisons du ski.*
**jouer + au/à la/aux** — play (competitive games, team sports)
*Il joue au football. Nous jouons aux échecs. Vous jouez au tennis.*
The distinction: *faire* is doing; *jouer* is playing against/with someone.

*Screen 5 — Cultural Activities (vocabulary)*
- **aller au cinéma** — to go to the cinema (France is the world's biggest cinema-going nation per capita)
- **aller à un concert** — to go to a concert
- **visiter un musée** — to visit a museum
- **lire un roman** — to read a novel
- **écouter de la musique** — to listen to music
- **regarder une série** — to watch a series

*Screen 6 — The French Cinema (narrative)*
France invented cinema: the Lumière brothers held their first commercial film screening in the Salon Indien du Grand Café, Paris, on December 28, 1895. The ticket cost one franc. Twenty-eight people attended. France has never let go of the claim. The French cinema industry is protected by the *exception culturelle* — tax on cinema tickets subsidizes French film production. French cinema has the *César* awards (the French Oscars). The *nouvelle vague* (New Wave) of the 1950s-60s — Godard, Truffaut, Varda — changed world cinema's vocabulary.

*Screen 7 — Expressing Preferences (concept)*
- **J'aime...** — I like...
- **J'adore...** — I love...
- **Je préfère...** — I prefer...
- **Je n'aime pas...** — I don't like...
- **Je déteste...** — I hate...
- **Ça m'intéresse.** — It interests me. (*intéresser* — to interest, takes indirect object)
- **Ça ne m'intéresse pas.** — It doesn't interest me.

*Screen 8 — Pétanque (cultural slide)*
*La pétanque* — the game played with metal boules thrown toward a small target ball (*le cochonnet*, the little pig) — is the most French of French leisure activities. Invented in La Ciotat, Provence, in 1907. Played on any flat gravel or dirt surface. The rules are simple; the culture around it is intricate — arguments about whose boule is closest are resolved with a measuring tape and a seriousness that the situation does not seem to require. Pétanque is played by the old and the young, in parks, on beaches, and in village squares. It is democratic leisure at its most French.

*Screen 9 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Qu'est-ce que tu fais le week-end ?** — What do you do on weekends?
- **J'aime beaucoup la randonnée en montagne.** — I really like hiking in the mountains.
- **Je joue au tennis le mercredi soir.** — I play tennis on Wednesday evenings.
- **Nous allons souvent au cinéma le dimanche.** — We often go to the cinema on Sundays.
- **Il préfère rester chez lui à lire.** — He prefers to stay home and read.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- *Faire du/de la* for sports you practice; *jouer au/à la* for games you play.
- The French *exception culturelle* protects cinema, music, and publishing.
- Pétanque: invented 1907, played on all surfaces, argued about seriously.
- Leisure in France is constitutionally adjacent to a right — it is legally protected.

**Grammar Point:** Object Pronouns with *Intéresser*, *Plaire*, *Manquer*
These three verbs reverse the English structure:
- *Ça m'intéresse* = That interests me (French: *ça* is subject; *me* is object)
- *Ça me plaît* = I like that (literally: "that pleases me")
- *Paris me manque* = I miss Paris (literally: "Paris is missing to me")
The thing acts; the person is acted upon. French reverses the subject-object of feeling.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Tu me manques* — "I miss you" — is one of the most misunderstood sentences in French for English speakers. *You* are the grammatical subject. *Me* is the object. Literally: "You are missing to me." The sentence says that *you* have created a lack in *me* — you are the active force even in my longing. This is either romantic philosophy or French grammar. Possibly both.

## Lesson 10 — Le Passé Composé: Narrating the Past

**Level:** A2 | **Theme:** Past Actions
**Central Insight:** The passé composé is not just a grammatical tense — it is the way French organizes human time into completed acts, and the choice of auxiliary verb (*être* or *avoir*) forces a classification of every action in the language.

**Cultural Thread:**
Proust's great novel *À la recherche du temps perdu* (In Search of Lost Time, 1913-1927) is entirely about the relationship between voluntary and involuntary memory — the famous madeleine dipped in tea that unlocks a world. The French passé composé — the compound past — is itself a philosophical structure: it is built from an auxiliary verb (*avoir* or *être*, which are in the present) and a past participle. The tense is literally "composed" — assembled from present and past, auxiliary and participle. To use it is to hold the past *in* the present: the action is over, but the telling of it happens now. This is memory made grammatical.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Le Passé Composé — Memory Made Grammatical*
"Proust spent seven volumes chasing a single moment: the past that lives in the present. The passé composé is how French grammatically does what Proust did literarily — it holds the completed past inside the present moment of speaking."

*Screen 2 — Structure (concept)*
Formula: **[Auxiliary] + [Past Participle]**
- *j'ai mangé* — I ate / I have eaten
- *il est parti* — he left / he has left

Auxiliary = *avoir* or *être* (present tense)
Past Participle = verb in its completed form:
- *-er* verbs → *-é*: *manger → mangé*
- *-ir* verbs → *-i*: *finir → fini*
- *-re* verbs → *-u*: *vendre → vendu*
Irregular: *être → été, avoir → eu, faire → fait, prendre → pris, venir → venu, vouloir → voulu*

*Screen 3 — Avoir vs Être: The Great Divide (concept)*
Most verbs use **avoir**: *j'ai mangé, j'ai travaillé, j'ai vu*
A group uses **être**: movement and state-change verbs + all reflexive verbs
The DR MRS P VAN DER TRAMP mnemonic covers the être verbs:
**D**evenir (become), **R**evenir (come back), **M**onter (go up), **R**ester (stay), **S**ortir (go out), **P**artir (leave), **V**enir (come), **A**ller (go), **N**aître (be born), **D**escendre (go down), **E**ntrer (enter), **R**entrer (return home), **T**omber (fall), **R**etourner (return), **A**rriver (arrive), **M**ourir (die), **P**asser (pass)

*Screen 4 — Agreement with Être (concept)*
When the auxiliary is *être*, the past participle **agrees with the subject** like an adjective:
- *Elle est **partie**.* — She left. (*-e* added for feminine)
- *Ils sont **partis**.* — They left. (*-s* added for masculine plural)
- *Elles sont **parties**.* — They (f.) left. (*-es* for feminine plural)
This agreement is visible in writing, often inaudible in speech. It is the ghost of Latin case endings.

*Screen 5 — Negation in Passé Composé (concept)*
Negation wraps around the auxiliary:
*Je n'ai pas mangé.* — I didn't eat. / I haven't eaten.
*Elle n'est pas venue.* — She didn't come.
The *ne... pas* sandwiches the auxiliary, not the past participle. The structure: **[ne] + [auxiliary] + [pas] + [participle]**.

*Screen 6 — Time Expressions for the Past (vocabulary)*
- **hier** — yesterday
- **avant-hier** — the day before yesterday
- **la semaine dernière** — last week
- **l'année dernière** — last year
- **il y a + [time]** — [time] ago (*il y a trois jours* = three days ago)
- **ce matin** — this morning (now past)
- **tout à l'heure** — a little while ago (also: in a little while — context decides)

*Screen 7 — Raconter (Narrating) (examples)*
- **Hier, je suis allé au marché et j'ai acheté du fromage.** — Yesterday, I went to the market and bought cheese.
- **Elle est née en 1985 à Bordeaux.** — She was born in 1985 in Bordeaux.
- **Nous avons vu un film formidable ce week-end.** — We saw a wonderful film this weekend.
- **Il y a deux ans, il est venu en France pour la première fois.** — Two years ago, he came to France for the first time.

*Screen 8 — Passé Composé vs Imparfait: The First Hint (concept)*
The passé composé is for **completed events** — actions that began and ended.
The imparfait (studied at B1) is for **descriptions, habits, and ongoing past states**.
For now: if you are narrating a specific event ("I went, I saw, I ate"), use passé composé. This is the narrative tense of spoken French.

*Screen 9 — Oral vs Written Register (concept)*
In formal written French, the passé composé is sometimes replaced by the **passé simple** — the literary past tense. You will not need to produce the passé simple until C1-C2, but you should know it exists: *je fis, il alla, elle fut* instead of *j'ai fait, il est allé, elle a été*. The passé simple exists in books, journalism, and formal writing. The passé composé exists in your mouth.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- Passé composé = [avoir/être present] + [past participle].
- Most verbs: *avoir*. Movement/state-change + reflexive: *être*.
- *Être* verbs: past participle agrees with subject.
- Negation: *ne... pas* wraps the auxiliary.
- It is the spoken narrative tense of French — use it confidently.

**Grammar Point:** Past Participle Formation
- *-er* → *-é*: regular and predictable.
- *-ir* → *-i*: mostly regular.
- *-re* → *-u*: mostly regular.
- Irregulars to learn by heart: *être/été, avoir/eu, faire/fait, dire/dit, écrire/écrit, lire/lu, mettre/mis, prendre/pris, voir/vu, vouloir/voulu, pouvoir/pu, savoir/su, venir/venu, naître/né, mourir/mort*.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Il y a* — used as "ago" — is one of French's most beautiful grammatical constructions. *Il y a trois jours* means three days ago, but literally it means "there are three days" — the distance in time described as a distance in space. Time as landscape. You are three days away from the event, the way you are three kilometers from a town. This is how French instinctively maps time.
