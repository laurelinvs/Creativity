## Rules That Feel Like Revelations

> *"La grammaire, c'est la logique de la langue."*
> — Traditional French school maxim

This is not a grammar textbook. Each entry is written as a revelation — the kind of explanation that makes a rule feel inevitable rather than arbitrary. Because French grammar rarely is arbitrary. There is always a reason: Latin evolution, phonetic pressure, social history, or philosophical preference. These are those reasons.

## A1 — FONDATIONS

---

### GR-01 — The Two Genders: Why Everything Has a Sex

**The Rule:** Every French noun is either masculine or feminine. There is no neutral gender.

**Why It Exists:** Latin had three genders: masculine, feminine, and neuter. As Latin evolved into Old French over five centuries, the neuter gender gradually disappeared, its nouns absorbed into either masculine or feminine. The system simplified from three to two — but the assignments from Latin carried over, sometimes with the original logic (*le soleil* — the sun — was masculine in Latin *sol*; *la lune* — the moon — was feminine in Luna), sometimes not.

There is no reliable rule from meaning. *La masculinité* (masculinity) is feminine. *Le féminisme* (feminism) is masculine. *Un vagin* is masculine; *un pénis* is also masculine (and they're both *-in* words, so the suffix doesn't help you). The gender system is historical, not logical — you learn it word by word.

**Patterns that help:**
- Endings in *-age, -ment, -oir, -eau* → usually masculine
- Endings in *-tion, -sion, -ure, -ette, -ance, -ence* → usually feminine
- Words ending in *-e* → slightly more likely to be feminine (but many exceptions)

**The Note:** The gender of a noun determines the form of every adjective, article, and participle that accompanies it. Getting the gender wrong doesn't just affect one word — it ripples through the entire sentence. Native speakers hear gender mismatches instantly. Learning genders with nouns from the beginning is not optional — it is the single most efficient investment of time in early French learning.

**Examples:**
- *le livre* (m.) — the book / *la librairie* (f.) — the bookshop
- *le problème* (m.) — the problem / *la solution* (f.) — the solution

### GR-02 — The Definite Article: Why *Le, La, Les* Are Not Just "The"

**The Rule:** *Le* (m.), *la* (f.), *l'* (before vowel/h), *les* (plural) — the definite article.

**Why It Exists:** Latin had no articles. Classical Latin said *canis* (dog), not *the dog*. As Latin evolved through vulgar speech into Old French, demonstrative pronouns (*ille, illa* — that one) were attached to nouns so frequently that they became reduced to articles. *Ille canem vidit* → *il a vu le chien*. The article evolved from a pointer.

**Beyond "the":** In French, the definite article is used far more broadly than in English:
- **Generic nouns:** *J'aime le café.* (I like coffee — in general.) English: "I like coffee." French requires *le*.
- **Abstract nouns:** *La liberté est un droit fondamental.* (Liberty is a fundamental right.) French requires the article.
- **Countries and regions:** *La France, l'Italie, les États-Unis, le Japon* — all take the definite article.
- **Languages:** *Je parle le français* — but after *parler*, the article is often dropped: *je parle français*.
- **Days of the week for habitual action:** *Le lundi, je travaille.* (On Mondays, I work — habitually.)

**The Note:** English-speakers consistently under-use the French definite article. The check: if you are making a general statement about a thing, you almost certainly need *le, la, les* in French even where English uses nothing.

### GR-03 — The Indefinite Article: *Un, Une, Des* — The Article of Introduction

**The Rule:** *Un* (m.), *une* (f.), *des* (plural) — a/an, some.

**After negation:** *Un/une/des* → *de/d'*:
*J'ai un livre.* → *Je n'ai pas de livre.*
*Il y a des pommes.* → *Il n'y a pas de pommes.*

**Why this change?** After negation, French grammar historically felt that the indefinite article was unnecessary — you are saying zero of something, so the unit marker *un* is removed. What remains is the partitive *de* — the "of" that signals an unquantified relation to the noun.

**The Note:** One exception that trips everyone: **adjective before plural noun** = *de/d'* (not *des*):
*Des livres intéressants* → *de bons livres* (good books — adjective first, so *de* replaces *des*)
This rule is followed in writing; in informal speech, *des bons livres* is increasingly heard.

### GR-04 — Present Tense: The Architecture of French Verb Groups

**The Rule:** French verbs fall into three groups: *-er* (regular), *-ir* (regular *-iss-* type), and everything else (*-re* verbs, irregular verbs).

**Group 1 (-er):** *parler* → *je parle, tu parles, il parle, nous parlons, vous parlez, ils parlent*
Final consonant is silent in singular; pronounced in plural (*parlons, parlez, parlent* — the *-ent* is silent). The difference between *il parle* and *ils parlent* is **only orthographic** in speech.

**Group 2 (-ir with -iss-):** *finir* → *je finis, tu finis, il finit, nous finissons, vous finissez, ils finissent*
The *-iss-* insertion in plural distinguishes these from the *venir/partir* type.

**Irregular verbs:** *être, avoir, aller, faire, pouvoir, vouloir, savoir, voir, venir* — each irregular, each essential, each memorized separately.

**Why the irregularities exist:** The most frequently used verbs are precisely the ones that resisted regularization — because they were used too often to change through analogy. The verbs *être* and *avoir* are so grammatically essential that their irregular forms are the oldest, most resistant elements of the language.

**The Note:** French has six persons but often only three or four distinct sounds. *Je parle, tu parles, il parle, ils parlent* are all pronounced identically. The written distinctions are orthographic records of Latin distinctions that pronunciation has erased. This is why French spelling can seem disproportionately complex — it is preserving historical distinctions that speech no longer makes.

### GR-05 — Tu and Vous: The Grammar of Distance

**The Rule:** *Tu* = second person singular informal. *Vous* = second person singular formal, or second person plural.

**Why It Exists:** Latin had *tu* (singular) and *vos* (plural). By the 3rd century, the Roman emperors began to be addressed in the plural *vos* — the imperial plural of majesty. The practice spread: addressing a person of higher status in the plural was a mark of deference. By medieval French, *vous* to a single person was standard in any asymmetric relationship. *Tu* became intimate, equal, or condescending (to servants and children).

The French Revolution briefly tried to abolish *vous* and impose universal *tu* as a marker of Republican equality — *citoyen, tu* for everyone. The effort failed. *Vous* came back with the Restoration and never left.

**What the choice reveals:** The *tu/vous* binary encodes the entire social map. Getting it wrong is not a grammatical error — it is a social signal. See the Secret Lesson on *Le Tutoiement* for the full cultural depth.

### GR-06 — Reflexive Verbs: When the Subject Acts on Itself

**The Rule:** Reflexive verbs use a reflexive pronoun (*me, te, se, nous, vous, se*) — the subject and object are the same entity.

**Examples:**
*je me lave* (I wash myself), *tu te lèves* (you get up), *il se souvient* (he remembers), *nous nous parlons* (we talk to each other)

**Why French has so many:** French uses the reflexive construction far more widely than English. Many French verbs are reflexive that have no English equivalent:
- *se souvenir de* — to remember (literally: to re-come to oneself — *souvenir* = memory)
- *se rendre compte* — to realize (to render to oneself an account)
- *se passer* — to happen (to pass oneself)
- *s'en aller* — to go away (to go oneself away)

The reflexive often marks the internalization of an action — what happens *to* the self rather than what the self does to others.

**The Note:** In passé composé, reflexive verbs always use *être*. And the past participle must agree with the reflexive pronoun when it is the direct object: *elle s'est lavée* (she washed herself — *se* = direct object, feminine agreement). But: *elle s'est lavé les mains* (she washed her hands — *les mains* is the direct object, not *se*; no agreement).

### GR-07 — Negation: The Double System

**The Rule:** Standard French negation uses two parts: *ne* before the verb and a second particle after (*pas, plus, jamais, rien, personne, que, guère, point, nullement*).

**The particles:**
- *ne... pas* — not
- *ne... plus* — no longer
- *ne... jamais* — never
- *ne... rien* — nothing
- *ne... personne* — no one
- *ne... que* — only (not actually a negation — a restriction)
- *ne... guère* — hardly (formal/literary)
- *ne... point* — not at all (literary/archaic)

**Why two parts?** Latin negation used *non* before the verb. Old French kept *ne* (from *non*) but added emphatic reinforcers: *pas* (a step — "not a step"), *point* (a point — "not a point"), *mie* (a crumb — "not a crumb"). These emphatic particles were originally comic exaggerations. Over time, the emphatic became standard and the bare *ne* insufficient alone.

**The colloquial reality:** In informal speech, *ne* is routinely dropped: *j'sais pas, il vient pas, elle a rien dit*. The *ne* survives in writing and formal speech. At A1, keep both. At B1, start hearing how French people actually speak.

### GR-08 — Questions: The Three Systems

**The Rule:** French has three ways to ask a question, in descending order of formality.

1. **Intonation** (informal): *Tu parles français ?* — Statement + rising intonation.
2. **Est-ce que** (neutral): *Est-ce que tu parles français ?* — The *est-ce que* prefix signals a question. Always correct; never too formal.
3. **Inversion** (formal): *Parles-tu français ?* — Subject and verb inverted; hyphen required. Formal register; used in written French, official speech, job interviews.

**The special case of "il/elle/on" inversion:** A phonetic rule requires inserting *-t-* between verb and pronoun when the verb ends in a vowel: *Parle-t-il ?* (Does he speak?) / *Aime-t-elle ?* (Does she like?). The *-t-* has no meaning — it prevents vowel collision.

**The Note:** The inversion question is heard less and less in everyday speech, even in educated register. *Est-ce que* is fully standard in all registers except the most formal writing. Many French speakers in casual conversation use only intonation.

### GR-09 — The Partitive Article: *Du, De la, Des*

**The Rule:** Use the partitive article to talk about an unspecified quantity of something uncountable.

*du pain* (some bread), *de la musique* (some music), *de l'eau* (some water), *des haricots* (some beans)

**After negation:** Always *de/d'*: *je n'ai pas de pain, je ne bois pas d'eau*.

**Why It Exists:** In Latin, you simply said *vinum* (wine). In French, to say "some wine" — a portion of the whole — a specific article evolved from *de + definite article*: *de + le → du, de + la → de la*. The partitive marks the indeterminate portion. It is one of French grammar's most useful concepts and one of the most systematically avoided by English-speakers who haven't been taught it.

**The Note:** The partitive *des* often collides with the indefinite article *des* (some/plural). They look identical but come from different logical origins: *des enfants* (some children — several units) vs *des légumes dans la soupe* (some vegetables in the soup — unspecified quantity of uncountable thing). Context usually distinguishes them.

### GR-10 — *C'est* vs *Il est/Elle est*: One of French's Most Useful Distinctions

**The Rule:**
- *C'est + noun/name/adjective + noun* — identifies, categorizes: *C'est un médecin. C'est Paris. C'est magnifique* (used with adjective when no specific subject is named).
- *Il est / Elle est + adjective* — describes a characteristic: *Il est médecin. Elle est belle. Il est content.*

**The crucial distinction with professions:**
- *C'est un médecin.* — He/she is a doctor (emphasizing the category)
- *Il est médecin.* — He is a doctor (stating a characteristic — no article before the profession)

After *il est / elle est*, professions take no article in standard French.

**The Note:** In informal spoken French, *c'est* is increasingly used where formal French uses *il est*: *c'est intéressant* instead of *il est intéressant* (for an impersonal subject). At A1, the distinction matters. At B2, you'll know when you can slide.

## A2 — ENVIRONNEMENT

---

### GR-11 — Passé Composé: The Perfect Tense That Replaced the Simple Past

**The Rule:** Passé composé = *avoir* or *être* + past participle. Used for completed past actions and events.

**Auxiliary choice:** *Avoir* for most verbs; *être* for a set of movement/state-change verbs (*aller, venir, partir, arriver, entrer, sortir, naître, mourir, rester, tomber, monter, descendre, passer, retourner, rentrer* + reflexive verbs).

**The mnemonic for *être* verbs:** *DR & MRS VANDERTRAMP* — or, more elegantly, think of them as verbs of **coming, going, remaining, and being born/dying**. They describe movement through space or the beginning/end of existence.

**Past participle agreement with *être*:** The past participle agrees with the subject in gender and number: *elle est partie, ils sont arrivés, elles se sont levées*.

**Why passé composé replaced the passé simple:** In classical and literary French, the *passé simple* (*je parlai, il vint*) was the narrative tense. Over the 18th and 19th centuries, *le passé composé* invaded everyday speech and informal writing. Today, the passé simple is used only in written literature and journalism — in conversation, passé composé has entirely taken its place.

---

### GR-12 — Imparfait: The Tense of Ongoing States

**The Rule:** Imparfait = *nous*-form stem + *-ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient*. Expresses: ongoing states, habitual past actions, background description, progressive past.

**The contrast:** Passé composé = specific completed event. Imparfait = background, state, habit.
*Il pleuvait* (it was raining — ongoing state) *quand elle est arrivée* (when she arrived — specific event).

**The Proust test:** Involuntary memories often use imparfait: *Quand j'étais enfant, ma mère préparait... nous mangions... c'était...* The imparfait gives a dreamlike quality to past description because it doesn't close an event — it holds it open.

**Exception:** *Être* forms its imparfait irregularly from *ét-*: *j'étais, tu étais, il était, nous étions, vous étiez, ils étaient*.

---

### GR-13 — Futur Simple vs Futur Proche

**The Rule:** Two ways to express future:
- **Futur proche** (*aller* + infinitive): *Je vais partir.* — imminent, planned, intended action
- **Futur simple** (infinitive + endings): *Je partirai.* — prediction, promise, less certain future

**Futur simple endings:** *-ai, -as, -a, -ons, -ez, -ont* added to the infinitive (verbs ending in *-re* drop the *e*: *prendre → je prendrai*).

**The cultural choice:** The futur proche is heavily preferred in spoken French. The futur simple adds a note of formality, certainty, or remoteness. A politician says *je ferai* (I will do) — it carries conviction. A friend says *je vais faire* (I'm going to do) — it's a plan.

### GR-14 — Pronouns: Pulling the Architecture Together

**The Rule:** French has a complex pronoun system. By A2, you need subject, direct object, indirect object, stressed, and *y/en*.

**Direct object:** *me, te, le/la, nous, vous, les* — replace direct objects:
*Je le vois. Je la connais. Je les aime.*

**Indirect object:** *me, te, lui, nous, vous, leur* — replace indirect objects (objects of *à + person*):
*Je lui parle. Je leur écris.*

**The distinction that trips everyone:** *lui* = indirect object for both masculine and feminine persons. *Je lui parle* could mean "I talk to him" or "I talk to her" — only context distinguishes.

**Y and en:**
- *Y* replaces *à + place* or *à + non-person*: *Je vais à Paris → J'y vais. Il pense à son travail → Il y pense.*
- *En* replaces *de + noun* or a partitive: *Je reviens de Lyon → J'en reviens. Je mange des pommes → J'en mange.*

---

### GR-15 — Adjective Agreement: The French Harmony of Description

**The Rule:** Adjectives agree in gender and number with the nouns they modify.

**Standard patterns:**
- Masculine: *grand, petit, bon*
- Feminine: *grande, petite, bonne* (add *-e*, which makes the final consonant audible)
- Plural: *grands, petits, bons* (add *-s*, usually silent)
- Feminine plural: *grandes, petites, bonnes*

**Irregular patterns:** *beau/belle/beaux/belles; nouveau/nouvelle; vieux/vieille; blanc/blanche; doux/douce; long/longue; public/publique*

**Position matters:** Most adjectives follow the noun in French: *une maison blanche, un problème difficile*. A small set of common adjectives precede: *un grand homme, un beau jardin, un petit problème, un vieux film*.

Some adjectives change meaning based on position: *un ancien professeur* (a former teacher) vs *un professeur ancien* (an old/ancient teacher); *un grand homme* (a great man) vs *un homme grand* (a tall man).

---

### GR-16 — Comparatives and Superlatives

**The Rule:**
- **Comparative:** *plus + adj + que* (more than), *moins + adj + que* (less than), *aussi + adj + que* (as ... as)
  *Elle est plus grande que lui. Il est moins rapide qu'elle. Tu es aussi intelligent qu'eux.*
- **Superlative:** *le/la/les + plus/moins + adjective*
  *C'est la plus belle ville de France. C'est le film le moins intéressant de l'année.*

**Irregular forms:**
- *bon → meilleur* (better / the best — not *plus bon*)
- *mauvais → pire* (worse / the worst — not *plus mauvais*, though used informally)
- *bien → mieux* (better, adverb)

**The Note:** *Meilleur* is adjectival (agrees); *mieux* is adverbial (invariable). *C'est meilleur* (it tastes better — flavor) vs *ça va mieux* (it's better — state/health). Confusing them marks the learner at every level.

## B1 — SPHÈRE SOCIALE

---

### GR-17 — Subjonctif Présent: The Mood of the Non-Real

**The Rule:** The subjunctive is required after expressions of desire, doubt, emotion, obligation, and possibility — when the action in the subordinate clause is not a factual assertion.

**Formation:** *nous*-form → remove *-ons* → add *-e, -es, -e, -ions, -iez, -ent*
*parler → nous parlons → parl- → que je parle, que tu parles, qu'il parle, que nous parlions...*

**Triggers:**
- Desire: *je veux que, je souhaite que, j'aimerais que*
- Doubt: *je doute que, il est peu probable que, je ne pense pas que*
- Emotion: *je suis content(e) que, il est dommage que, j'ai peur que*
- Obligation: *il faut que, il est nécessaire que, il est essentiel que*
- Concession: *bien que, quoique, encore que*

**The crucial distinction:** After *je pense que* (affirmative) → indicative. After *je ne pense pas que* (negative) → subjunctive. The negative creates doubt; the doubt triggers the subjunctive.

**Irregular subjunctives to memorize:** *être → que je sois, que tu sois, qu'il soit*; *avoir → que j'aie, que tu aies, qu'il ait*; *aller → que j'aille*; *faire → que je fasse*; *pouvoir → que je puisse*; *savoir → que je sache*; *vouloir → que je veuille*.

---

### GR-18 — The Subjunctive in Relative Clauses (Introduction)

**The Rule:** When the antecedent of a relative clause is hypothetical or sought but not found, the subjunctive is used.

*Je cherche quelqu'un qui **sache** le russe.* — I'm looking for someone who knows Russian. (If such a person exists.)
*J'ai trouvé quelqu'un qui **sait** le russe.* — I found someone who knows Russian. (This person exists.)

The subjunctive marks the *unrealized* — the thing that might or might not exist. The indicative marks the *realized* — the thing that exists.

---

### GR-19 — Indirect Speech: Reporting What Was Said

**The Rule:** When reporting speech in French, tenses shift backward in time.

Direct: *Il a dit: "Je suis fatigué."*
Indirect: *Il a dit qu'il était fatigué.* (présent → imparfait)

Direct: *Il a dit: "Je suis allé au cinéma."*
Indirect: *Il a dit qu'il était allé au cinéma.* (passé composé → plus-que-parfait)

Direct: *Il a dit: "Je partirai demain."*
Indirect: *Il a dit qu'il partirait le lendemain.* (futur → conditionnel; *demain* → *le lendemain*)

**Time expressions also shift:** *hier → la veille, demain → le lendemain, maintenant → alors, aujourd'hui → ce jour-là*

---

### GR-20 — The Gerund: *En* + Present Participle

**The Rule:** *En + -ant* form — the gerund — expresses simultaneous action, manner, or condition.

*En travaillant dur, tu réussiras.* (By working hard / if you work hard)
*Il lit en mangeant.* (He reads while eating)
*Elle est entrée en courant.* (She entered running)

**Formation:** *nous*-form → *-ons* → *-ant*
*travaillons → travaillant; finissons → finissant; prenons → prenant*

**Exceptions:** *être → étant; avoir → ayant; savoir → sachant*

The gerund is invariable — it never agrees with anything. One form, used always.

---

### GR-21 — Relative Pronouns: *Qui, Que, Dont, Où*

**The Rule:**
- *Qui* = subject of the relative clause: *le livre **qui** est sur la table*
- *Que* = direct object: *le livre **que** j'ai lu*
- *Dont* = replaces *de + noun*: *le livre **dont** je t'ai parlé* (*parler de*)
- *Où* = place or time: *la ville **où** je suis né, le moment **où** il est parti*

**The *dont* key:** Identify the verb that uses *de* in the relative clause. If the verb is *avoir besoin de, parler de, se souvenir de, être fier de, avoir peur de* — use *dont*.

*L'histoire **dont** je me souviens.* (from *se souvenir de*)
*Le problème **dont** nous avons parlé.* (from *parler de*)

**Lequel, laquelle, lesquels, lesquelles:** Replaces a noun after a preposition (other than *de* → *dont* or *à* → *où* for place). *La raison **pour laquelle** je suis venu.* (The reason for which I came.)

---

### GR-22 — The Conditional: Politeness, Hypothesis, and the Journalistic Distance

**The Rule:** Conditional = infinitive + imparfait endings.
*je parlerais, tu parlerais, il parlerait, nous parlerions, vous parleriez, ils parleraient*

**Irregular stems:** Same stems as futur simple + imparfait endings: *avoir → j'aurais, être → je serais, aller → j'irais, faire → je ferais, venir → je viendrais, pouvoir → je pourrais, vouloir → je voudrais*

**Uses:**
1. Polite requests: *Je voudrais un café.* (softer than *je veux*)
2. Hypothesis (*si + imparfait → conditional*): *Si j'avais le temps, je voyagerais.*
3. Advice: *Tu devrais lui parler.*
4. Journalistic distance: *Le gouvernement **aurait** décidé...* (allegedly decided — not verified)

**Never:** conditional after *si* (the hypothetical conjunction). *Si je serais...* is an error.

---

### GR-23 — *Depuis*, Duration, and the Present/Imparfait

**The Rule:**
- *Depuis + présent* — action begun in the past, continuing now: *J'étudie le français depuis deux ans.* (I've been studying French for two years — and still am.)
- *Depuis + imparfait* — past action that was ongoing at a given past moment: *J'étudiais le français depuis deux ans quand j'ai visité Paris.* (I had been studying French for two years when I visited Paris.)
- *Ça fait + duration + que + present*: *Ça fait deux ans que j'étudie le français.* (Same meaning as *depuis* + present.)

**The English trap:** English uses perfect/pluperfect. French uses present/imparfait. The action is ongoing from the French perspective, so it stays in the present tense.

## B2 — PROFESSIONNEL

---

### GR-24 — Nominalization: The Formal Style Engine

**The Rule:** Nominalization transforms verb or adjective phrases into noun phrases.

*On a décidé de réformer* → *la décision de réformer*
*La situation s'améliore* → *l'amélioration de la situation*
*On va analyser* → *une analyse de*

**Why it exists:** Nominalizations compress action into nouns, making statements denser, more abstract, and harder to challenge. Bureaucratic, legal, and academic French use heavy nominalization because it depersonalizes — the decision was made (by nobody in particular) rather than *we decided* (somebody specific, accountable).

**The aesthetics:** Flaubert avoided heavy nominalization; Proust loved it. Sartre's prose is highly nominalized; Camus's is not. Nominalization is not better or worse — it is a choice with consequences.

---

### GR-25 — The Plus-Que-Parfait: The Past Behind the Past

**The Rule:** Plus-que-parfait (pluperfect) = *avoir/être* (imparfait) + past participle.
Expresses: an action that occurred before another past action.

*Elle était déjà partie quand je suis arrivé.* (She had already left when I arrived.)
*Il avait mangé avant de sortir.* (He had eaten before going out.)

**Why it matters:** It establishes temporal sequence in complex narratives. Without the pluperfect, the order of events in a story becomes ambiguous. With it, the earlier event is unambiguously prior.

**In *si* clauses for past counterfactuals:**
*Si je l'avais su, j'aurais agi différemment.* (If I had known, I would have acted differently.)
This is the most complex *si* construction — passé composé → plus-que-parfait.

---

### GR-26 — Passive Voice and French Alternatives

**The Rule:** Passive = *être* (any tense) + past participle (agrees with subject):
*La loi a été votée.* (The law was passed.)
*Le projet sera présenté demain.* (The project will be presented tomorrow.)

**French alternatives:**
- *On* construction: *On a voté la loi.* (They passed the law.) Much preferred in speech.
- Reflexive: *Ça s'est passé hier.* (It happened yesterday — passive meaning via reflexive form.)
- Pronominal passive: *Ce livre se vend bien.* (This book sells well — literally "sells itself well.")

**Why avoid passive in French?** The passive voice in French is frequently considered awkward and bureaucratic. The *on* construction or a reflexive is stylistically preferred in most contexts. Write passive in formal documents; prefer active or *on* in prose.

---

### GR-27 — Si Clauses: The Full System

**The Rule:** Three tense combinations for three types of conditionality:

| Type | *Si* clause | Main clause | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Real | présent | futur | *Si tu viens, on sortira.* |
| Hypothetical | imparfait | conditionnel | *Si tu venais, on sortirait.* |
| Past counterfactual | plus-que-parfait | conditionnel passé | *Si tu étais venu, on serait sortis.* |

**The one absolute rule:** Never use the conditional or the future in the *si* clause. This error — *si je serais* — is the grammatical marker of every intermediate learner. Eliminate it.

---

### GR-28 — The Infinitive Construction: French's Alternative to the Gerund

**The Rule:** When two verbs share the same subject, French prefers *verb + infinitive*:

*Je veux partir.* (I want to leave — same subject *je* for both verbs.)
*Elle espère réussir.* (She hopes to succeed.)

This is different from English, where "I want to leave" is standard — but the French construction also covers cases where English uses a gerund:
*Je préfère marcher.* (I prefer walking / to walk.)
*Il adore chanter.* (He loves singing / to sing.)

**After prepositions:** *à, de, pour, sans, avant de, afin de, au lieu de* + infinitive:
*Il est parti sans dire au revoir.* (He left without saying goodbye.)
*Avant de partir, il a dit au revoir.* (Before leaving, he said goodbye.)

**The past infinitive:** *Après avoir mangé, il s'est couché.* (After eating/having eaten, he went to bed.) Used when the infinitive action is prior to the main verb.

## C1 — STRATÉGIQUE

---

### GR-29 — Rhetorical Inversion: Fronting for Effect

**The Rule:** In formal and literary French, adverbs and negative expressions placed at the beginning of a sentence trigger subject-verb inversion:

*Jamais il n'avait vu pareille chose.* (He had never seen such a thing.)
*Rarement se retrouve-t-on dans une telle situation.* (Rarely does one find oneself in such a situation.)
*À peine était-il arrivé que le téléphone sonna.* (He had barely arrived when the telephone rang.)

**The formal effect:** Inversion signals high register, carefully crafted style, or rhetorical intent. In political speeches, academic writing, and literary prose, fronted inversion is a mark of elegance.

---

### GR-30 — The Conditionnel Passé: Regret, Reproach, and the Past Counterfactual

**The Rule:** Conditionnel passé = *avoir/être* (conditionnel) + past participle.
*j'aurais parlé, il serait parti, elle se serait levée*

**Uses:**
1. Past counterfactual (*si + plus-que-parfait*): *Si j'avais su, je serais venu.*
2. Reproach: *Tu aurais dû me le dire.* (You should have told me.)
3. Regret: *J'aurais voulu être là.* (I would have liked to be there.)
4. Journalistic unconfirmed past: *Il aurait rencontré le président.* (He reportedly met the president.)

---

### GR-31 — *Ne* Without *Pas*: Expletive *Ne* and Literary Negation

**The Rule:** In formal and literary French, *ne* appears without *pas* in two main contexts:

**1. After expressions of fear:** *J'ai peur qu'il ne parte.* (I'm afraid he might leave — the *ne* signals undesirable possibility, not a real negation.)

**2. After comparisons:** *Il est plus grand qu'il ne le pensait.* (He is taller than he thought — the *ne* in the subordinate clause after a comparative is expletive — it adds no negation.)

**Why it exists:** The *ne* without *pas* is a survival of Old French, where *ne* alone was a full negation. The expletive use in comparisons and after fear expressions preserved the *ne* in contexts where the modern mind reads it as a quasi-negation — something slightly negative is implied.

---

### GR-32 — Complex Sentence Architecture: Subordination Strategies

**The Rule:** Advanced French sentences use multiple levels of subordination:

*C'est parce que les institutions républicaines reposent sur un consensus fragile qu'il convient de les renforcer avant que les pressions politiques ne viennent les ébranler.*

(It is because the Republican institutions rest on a fragile consensus that it is fitting to strengthen them before political pressures come to shake them.)

This sentence contains:
- *C'est... que* (emphasis structure)
- *parce que* + subjective clause with *reposent*
- *il convient de + infinitive*
- *avant que + subjonctif* (*ne viennent* — expletive ne)

**The skill:** At C1, recognize and produce these structures. Practice by analyzing the syntactic levels of complex sentences in French editorial writing.

---

### GR-33 — Nominative Inversion in Questions (Advanced)

**The Rule:** In formal written French, questions can place the lexical subject after the verb:

*Que pense le ministre de cette proposition ?* (What does the minister think of this proposal?)
*Quand viendra la décision du Conseil ?* (When will the Council's decision come?)

This inversion (verb before lexical subject) is distinct from the pronominal inversion (*pense-t-il ?*). It is standard in formal questions and interview headlines in French journalism.

## C2 — MAÎTRISE

---

### GR-34 — Le Passé Simple: The Literary Tense

**The Rule:** Passé simple = the narrative past of written French literature and journalism. In speech, entirely replaced by passé composé.

**Formation:**
- *-er* verbs: *je parlai, tu parlas, il parla, nous parlâmes, vous parlâtes, ils parlèrent*
- *-ir/-re* verbs: *je finis / je pris, tu finis / tu pris, il finit / il prit, nous finîmes / nous prîmes...*
- Irregular: *être → je fus; avoir → j'eus; faire → je fis; voir → je vis; venir → je vins*

**The tone:** The passé simple creates narrative distance. Reading it, you feel the events as completed, sealed, belonging to a finished world. Camus's *L'Étranger* uses passé composé throughout — its narrator speaks in his own time. Flaubert's *Madame Bovary* uses passé simple — the narrator stands outside the story. This choice is the writer's first major decision.

---

### GR-35 — Subjonctif Imparfait: The Literary Subjunctive

**The Rule:** The imparfait du subjonctif is the subjunctive tense used in literary French when the main clause is in the past.

**Formation from passé simple:** Take the 3rd person singular passé simple → remove the final letter → add subjunctive endings.

*parla → qu'il parlât* | *finit → qu'il finît* | *fut → qu'il fût* | *eût → qu'il eût*

**Example:** *Il craignait qu'elle ne **partît** sans lui dire au revoir.* (He feared she might leave without saying goodbye.)

Modern equivalent (indicatif): *Il craignait qu'elle **parte** sans lui dire au revoir.*

The subjunctive imparfait survives in literature, some formal writing, and self-consciously elevated style. Encountering it: you are reading something written with care.

---

### GR-36 — Style: The Sentence as Rhythm

**The Rule:** There is no rule. At C2, style is the application of all grammatical knowledge in the service of effect.

**Flaubert's principle:** The ideal French sentence has the prose rhythm of verse without the constraint of meter. He tested his sentences by reading them aloud in his *gueuloir* (shouting room). A sentence that could not be read aloud with pleasure was revised.

**Rhythm patterns:** 
- Binary rhythm: *il prit son manteau et sortit* — two-beat action, quick, decisive.
- Ternary rhythm: *il prit son manteau, regarda une dernière fois la chambre, et sortit* — three beats, more reflective, cinematic.
- Periodic sentence: subordinate clause, subordinate clause, subordinate clause — then the main clause. The French periodic sentence builds tension and releases it at the end.

The grammar of style is inaudible when it works. It is the thing the reader feels without knowing what they are feeling.
