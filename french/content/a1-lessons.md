# A1 — Fondations
## The First Ten Lessons

> *"Au commencement était le Verbe."*
> — John 1:1, as rendered in French — the first line every French schoolchild learns about language itself.

These ten lessons are the threshold. The learner standing at this door has never spoken French, or has spoken it badly, or has spoken it only in fear. The job of A1 is not to teach words — it is to dismantle the idea that French is difficult. It is difficult the way any profound thing is difficult: because it has a history, a logic, and a beauty that rewards attention. These lessons teach that attention.

Each lesson below contains: the central insight, the cultural thread, the teaching arc (ten screens), the primary grammar point, the vocabulary list, and the teacher's note — the thing no textbook says.

## Lesson 1 — La Parole: Learning to Say Hello

**Level:** A1 | **Theme:** Greetings & First Contact
**Central Insight:** *Bonjour* is not just a greeting. It is a social contract.

**Cultural Thread:**
In France, the greeting is obligatory in a way that surprises foreigners. Enter a small shop without saying *bonjour* and the shopkeeper will not be rude — but something will have gone slightly wrong. The room will know. A French *bonjour* acknowledges the other person's existence before anything else is transacted. It is inherited from the Latin *bonus dies* (good day), passed through the Middle Ages, compressed into a single word, and loaded with a meaning that goes beyond politeness into something closer to mutual recognition. The philosopher Emmanuel Levinas spent a career arguing that ethics begins with the face of the Other. The French *bonjour* has been practicing this since the 12th century.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening Scene (cover)*
Title: *La Parole — The Gift of Speech*
Visual concept: A Parisian café at 8am. A man in a coat enters. The barista looks up. One word passes between them. Everything is in order.
The text: "French has a word that contains an entire philosophy of human encounter. You already know it."

*Screen 2 — The Word (character slide)*
Word: **BONJOUR**
Pronunciation: bɔ̃.ʒuʁ — the nasal vowel *on* like the beginning of "bone" said through the nose, then *jour* rhymes with "sure" in British English, not "jaw."
Etymology: *bon* (good, from Latin *bonus*) + *jour* (day, from Latin *diurnus*, of the day). Originally two words. The compression into one happened gradually between the 13th and 16th centuries.
Teaching note: In French, the greeting is not optional. Entering a room, a shop, a meeting without *bonjour* is not neutral — it is a small act of erasure.

*Screen 3 — The Register Split (cards2)*
Left card: **BONJOUR** — Formal/universal. Use with anyone, any time of day until roughly 6pm. Safe in every situation.
Right card: **SALUT** — Informal. Friends, close colleagues, family. Never with a shopkeeper, a stranger, a doctor. *Salut* says: I already know you well enough.

*Screen 4 — Time Shifts the Greeting (cards3)*
Card 1: **BONJOUR** — Morning through late afternoon
Card 2: **BONSOIR** — Evening (from roughly 5-6pm onward)
Card 3: **BONNE NUIT** — Only when someone is going to sleep. Not a greeting — a farewell to the night.

*Screen 5 — The Departure (vocabulary)*
How to leave:
- **Au revoir** — Until we see each other again. The *voir* is "to see." Built on hope of return.
- **À bientôt** — See you soon. More warmth than *au revoir*.
- **À tout à l'heure** — See you in a bit (same day, within hours).
- **Bonne journée / Bonne soirée** — Have a good day / evening. Said when parting mid-interaction.

*Screen 6 — Politeness: The Essential Three (concept)*
Formula: REQUEST = [want/need] + **s'il vous plaît** / **s'il te plaît** + [merci]
- **S'il vous plaît** — "If it pleases you" (formal). Literally: *si* (if) + *il* (it) + *vous* (you, formal) + *plaît* (pleases, from plaire). The subjunctive hidden in plain sight.
- **S'il te plaît** — same, but with *te* (you, informal). The grammar of intimacy embedded in a courtesy phrase.
- **Merci** — from Latin *mercedem*, reward, wage. To thank someone was once to acknowledge a debt. The word carries that weight still.

*Screen 7 — The Art of Pardon (cards2)*
Left: **EXCUSEZ-MOI** — Excuse me. Used to get attention, pass someone on the street, interrupt politely. The *vous* form is standard even with strangers.
Right: **PARDON** — Sorry / pardon. More immediate than *excusez-moi*. Used when you've bumped into someone, stepped on a foot, interrupted mid-sentence. The English borrowed this word and kept it.

*Screen 8 — Yes and No (concept)*
**OUI** and **NON** — the most powerful short words in any language.
But in French: **SI** — the answer "yes" to a negative question. If someone says "Tu ne parles pas français?" (You don't speak French?), you don't say *oui* (which would confirm you don't speak it) — you say *SI*, the emphatic yes-in-contradiction. Latin: *sic*, meaning "thus, so." The French kept a word for a kind of yes that English doesn't have.

*Screen 9 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Bonjour, comment allez-vous ?** — Hello, how are you? (formal)
  → *Comment allez-vous* literally means "how do you go?" — French measures wellbeing by movement.
- **Ça va ?** — You OK? (informal) — the word *ça* is an abbreviation of *cela* (that). "That goes?"
- **Ça va, merci. Et vous ?** — Fine, thanks. And you?
- **Bonsoir, au revoir, et bonne nuit.** — Good evening, goodbye, and goodnight.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
What you've learned today:
- French greetings are not pleasantries — they are social acknowledgements that carry weight.
- *Bonjour* is the master key. It opens every door.
- Formality is a dial: *bonjour/au revoir* for anyone; *salut/à plus* for those you've chosen.
- *Si* is the yes that fights back.

**Grammar Point:** Personal Pronouns & Basic Verb Forms
*Rule: French verbs change form depending on who is acting. The verb "aller" (to go) in its question form reveals the formal "you": **allez-vous** (do you go / how are you?).*
In A1, you only need to know: *je* (I), *vous* (you-formal/plural), *tu* (you-informal). Everything else will come.

**Teacher's Note:**
The French do not say "I'm fine." They say *ça va* — "that goes." The entire national philosophy of emotional restraint is in that evasion. If someone asks *ça va?* and you say *très bien, merci* you have told them more than they expected. The authentic French answer to *ça va?* is another *ça va* — it's a greeting, not a question. Knowing this is worth more than any vocabulary list.

## Lesson 2 — Les Chiffres: Numbers and the Logic of Twenty

**Level:** A1 | **Theme:** Numbers 1–100
**Central Insight:** French numbers above sixty are not arbitrary — they reveal a counting system inherited from the Celts who counted in twenties.

**Cultural Thread:**
Every French child at some point asks why seventy is *soixante-dix* (sixty-ten) instead of something simpler. The answer is that France inherited two counting systems — the decimal Roman system and the vigesimal (base-20) Celtic one — and never fully resolved the conflict. Belgium and Switzerland, lacking the Parisian centralizing force of the Académie française, tidied up the problem and use *septante, huitante, nonante* (70, 80, 90). Metropolitan France kept the irrational forms because the Académie, founded in 1635 under Cardinal Richelieu, codified the forms as they existed in Paris. This is a lesson in how political centralization shapes language.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Compter — Counting the World*
The question: "Why is 80 in French literally 'four-twenties'? The answer involves Julius Caesar, Gallic tribes, and a cardinal who hated messy grammar."

*Screen 2 — 1 to 10 (vocabulary)*
**un, deux, trois, quatre, cinq, six, sept, huit, neuf, dix**
Pronunciation key: *trois* rhymes with "swah"; *six* is "sees" before a vowel, "sis" alone; *huit* is "weet" — the *h* is mute but prevents liaison.

*Screen 3 — 11 to 16: the fused numbers (concept)*
Formula: 11–16 are irregular → **onze, douze, treize, quatorze, quinze, seize**
These come directly from Latin: *undecim, duodecim, tredecim...* They were never rebuilt on a decimal pattern because they were already stable, embedded in daily life (the 12 months, the 14th of July, the 16th arrondissement).

*Screen 4 — 17 to 19: the logic reappears (cards3)*
**dix-sept** (10+7), **dix-huit** (10+8), **dix-neuf** (10+9)
Back to logic: after 16, French simply adds ten to the unit. This is the decimal system reasserting itself.

*Screen 5 — 20 to 60 (vocabulary)*
The regulars: **vingt, trente, quarante, cinquante, soixante**
Pattern: add *et un* (and one) for the -1 forms (*vingt et un, trente et un*...) but *vingt-deux, trente-deux* (hyphen, no *et*). The *et* is the ghost of Latin *et unum*, lingering only at the -one position.

*Screen 6 — The Celtic Surprise (concept)*
**70 = soixante-dix** (sixty-ten)
**80 = quatre-vingts** (four-twenties)
**90 = quatre-vingt-dix** (four-twenties-ten)
The Celtic Gauls counted in groups of 20 — *vingt* was their base unit. The Romans brought decimal counting; the two systems fused imperfectly. Paris standardized the Celtic forms. The rest of the francophone world largely didn't. This is the most visible trace of pre-Roman Gaul in modern French.

*Screen 7 — 100 and Beyond (concept)*
**cent** (100), **mille** (1000), **un million**, **un milliard**
Note: *cent* takes an *s* when multiplied and not followed by another number: *deux cents* but *deux cent cinquante*. This rule exists because the plural *s* was historically dropped before another numeral — a survival of medieval scribal practice.

*Screen 8 — Numbers in Real Life (examples)*
- **J'ai vingt-trois ans.** — I am 23 years old.
- **C'est le quatorze juillet.** — It's the fourteenth of July. (Bastille Day)
- **Le train part à seize heures quarante.** — The train leaves at 16:40. (France uses 24-hour time.)
- **Ça coûte quatre-vingt-cinq euros.** — It costs 85 euros.

*Screen 9 — Phone Numbers (narrative)*
French phone numbers are read in pairs: **06 12 34 56 78** is *zéro six, douze, trente-quatre, cinquante-six, soixante-dix-huit*. This is because French telephone systems were organized in pairs from their introduction in the early 20th century. A practical habit that becomes a cultural reflex.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
What you've learned:
- 1–16 irregular (from Latin), 17–19 decimal, 20–60 regular, 60+ vigesimal-Celtic.
- *Quatre-vingts* with an s when alone; without when followed by another number.
- France uses 24-hour clock in formal contexts (transport, appointments, official documents).
- Swiss and Belgian French "fixed" the system. French French preserved it. Both are right.

**Grammar Point:** Gender in Numbers
*Un* (one) has a feminine form: *une*. *Vingt et un / vingt et une*. Numbers agree in gender when modifying a noun — the only place numbers show gender in French.

**Teacher's Note:**
When a French person gives you their phone number, they will never say "six, zero, one, two..." — they will say it in pairs, probably fast, and expect you to keep up. Write in pairs. Also: the French word for the number 1 (*un/une*) is also the indefinite article. The number and the article are the same word. This is not a coincidence — Latin *unus* gave both.

## Lesson 3 — Au Café: The Art of Ordering

**Level:** A1 | **Theme:** Food, Drink & The Café
**Central Insight:** The French café is not a coffee shop. It is an institution with a social protocol as old as the Enlightenment.

**Cultural Thread:**
The Parisian café was born in the 17th century. The first, Café de Procope (1686, still open today), served as the meeting place of Voltaire, Rousseau, Diderot — the men who built the Enlightenment over coffee. The café became the public living room of French intellectual life: where revolutions were planned, novels written, and manifestos argued over. To order at a French café is not a commercial transaction — it is participation in a tradition. The waiter is not a servant; they are the keeper of the space. They have a word for their professional dignity: *le service*. Respecting it costs nothing and returns everything.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Le Café — France's Living Room*
The scene: Flore, Deux Magots, or the zinc bar of any brasserie. Existentialists, poets, politicians, and neighbourhood regulars — the café holds them all. What do you say when you sit down?

*Screen 2 — Essential Drinks (vocabulary)*
- **un café** — a small black espresso (not a large American filter coffee)
- **un café au lait** — espresso with hot milk (morning only — ordering this after noon is noticed)
- **un crème** — espresso with frothed milk (a Parisian specific)
- **un thé** — tea (usually a bag in hot water, not the British ritual)
- **une eau plate** — still water
- **une eau gazeuse** — sparkling water
- **un jus d'orange** — orange juice (freshly squeezed: *pressé*)
- **une bière** — beer (draft: *une pression*)

*Screen 3 — Essential Food (vocabulary)*
- **un croissant** — the crescent pastry, said to commemorate Vienna's victory over the Ottoman crescent in 1683 (probably apocryphal, but the French keep the story)
- **une tartine** — toasted baguette with butter
- **un sandwich jambon-beurre** — ham and butter baguette: the most-eaten sandwich in France
- **une quiche lorraine** — egg, cream, lardons — from Lorraine province, hence the name

*Screen 4 — The Order (concept)*
Formula: **Je voudrais + [item], s'il vous plaît.**
*Je voudrais* (I would like) — NOT *je veux* (I want). *Je veux* is what children say. *Je voudrais* is the conditional form — it softens the request into an expression of desire rather than a demand. This is A1 grammar doing adult work: the conditional tense protects politeness.

*Screen 5 — The Waiter Protocol (narrative)*
The French waiter will not hover. They will not come by to ask "how's everything tasting?" mid-bite. This is not indifference — it is respect for your meal. To get their attention: make brief eye contact, raise your hand slightly, say *Excusez-moi* or *S'il vous plaît* clearly. Do not snap fingers. Do not say *Garçon* (this is now considered rude, though it was standard until the 1980s).

*Screen 6 — Asking for the Bill (concept)*
**L'addition, s'il vous plaît.**
*L'addition* is the bill. Unlike in some cultures, the bill in France is never brought automatically — the waiter waits to be asked. This is intentional: the table is yours for as long as you need it. A French restaurant never "turns" a table. Asking for the bill is not rushing; it is asserting that you are done.

*Screen 7 — Paying (cards2)*
Left: **Service compris** — service (tip) included. Written on most bills. The 15% service charge is already in the price. French law requires this.
Right: **Laisser un pourboire** — leaving a tip. Optional, and genuinely optional. A few coins, or rounding up, is a gesture — not an obligation.

*Screen 8 — Real Exchanges (examples)*
- **Je voudrais un café et un croissant, s'il vous plaît.** — I'd like a coffee and a croissant.
- **Vous désirez ?** — What would you like? (what the waiter says)
- **C'est combien ?** — How much is it?
- **L'addition, s'il vous plaît.** — The bill, please.
- **C'est délicieux.** — It's delicious.

*Screen 9 — Bon Appétit (cultural slide)*
**Bon appétit** — the phrase said before eating. Unlike the Anglo-American tradition where grace or silence might precede meals, the French announcement of appetite before eating is a secular ritual, universal regardless of religion or class. To skip it is to be noticed. To say it is to join the table.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- *Je voudrais* (not *je veux*) to order.
- *L'addition s'il vous plaît* — the bill on request, never automatic.
- Service is already included. Tipping is optional.
- *Bon appétit* before eating. Always.
- An espresso (*un café*) is standard. If you want large: ask for *un café allongé* or *un grand café*.

**Grammar Point:** The Partitive Article — du, de la, des
When ordering food, French uses a special article that English doesn't have: the partitive.
- **du pain** — some bread (masculine: *du* = *de* + *le*)
- **de la confiture** — some jam (feminine)
- **des croissants** — some croissants (plural)
The partitive says: "an unspecified quantity of this thing." English uses "some" or nothing. French obligates you to specify.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Un café au lait* after noon marks you immediately as a tourist to a French waiter. After the morning meal, the French drink *un café* (espresso) or nothing. The milk in coffee is a breakfast custom — it soaks up the tartine, it launches the day. After that, the coffee stands alone. Ordering a *café au lait* at 3pm won't get you thrown out. But you'll know.

## Lesson 4 — La Famille: Family Names and What They Reveal

**Level:** A1 | **Theme:** Family & Relationships
**Central Insight:** French family vocabulary is not a neutral list — it encodes the Latin legal structure that shaped French society for two thousand years.

**Cultural Thread:**
The Roman *paterfamilias* — the father as legal and economic head of family — dominated French law until 1970. French family vocabulary still carries this architecture. The word for "parents" (*les parents*) means both parents and, in older usage, all relatives. The word for "children" (*les enfants*) is grammatically masculine plural even when describing a mixed group — the masculine absorbs the feminine in French grammar, a rule that became politically contested in the 20th century and led to the movement for *écriture inclusive* (inclusive writing). Learning the family vocabulary is learning a small piece of the debate about who French has historically centered.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *La Famille — The First Network*
The question that opens the lesson: "Why does French have a word for 'stepfather' (*beau-père*) that is literally 'beautiful father'? The history is in the etymology."

*Screen 2 — The Core Family (vocabulary)*
- **la mère** — mother (from Latin *mater*)
- **le père** — father (from Latin *pater*)
- **la sœur** — sister (from Latin *soror*)
- **le frère** — brother (from Latin *frater*)
- **les parents** — parents / all relatives
- **l'enfant** — child (from Latin *infans*, "without speech" — the pre-verbal being)

*Screen 3 — The Extended Family (vocabulary)*
- **la grand-mère** / **le grand-père** — grandmother / grandfather
- **la tante** / **l'oncle** — aunt / uncle
- **la cousine** / **le cousin** — female / male cousin
- **la nièce** / **le neveu** — niece / nephew
- **les grands-parents** — grandparents

*Screen 4 — The Beau- Trap (concept)*
In French, step-relations use *beau/belle* (beautiful):
- **le beau-père** — stepfather OR father-in-law
- **la belle-mère** — stepmother OR mother-in-law
- **le beau-fils** — stepson OR son-in-law
Context always clarifies. The etymology: *beau* here comes from Latin *bellus*, agreeable, chosen — the "added" family as opposed to the biological one. The word for in-law relationships is the same as for step-relationships. French encoded both experiences of "non-blood family member" in one word. This causes genuine confusion. Embrace it.

*Screen 5 — Gender in Family Words (grammar slide)*
French makes gender visible in family words:
- **le fils** (son) / **la fille** (daughter) — completely different words
- **le mari** (husband) / **la femme** (wife) — note that *femme* also means "woman"
- **le frère** / **la sœur** — different words (not feminized forms)
- **le cousin** / **la cousine** — the feminine adds *-e*, which changes the final sound (coo-ZAN → coo-ZEEN)

*Screen 6 — Talking About Family (examples)*
- **J'ai une sœur et deux frères.** — I have a sister and two brothers.
- **Ma mère s'appelle Marie.** — My mother's name is Marie.
- **Mes parents habitent à Lyon.** — My parents live in Lyon.
- **C'est mon oncle.** — That's my uncle.
- **Tu as des enfants ?** — Do you have children? (informal)

*Screen 7 — Possession with Family (concept)*
French possessive adjectives agree with the thing owned, not the owner:
- **mon père** (my father — masculine) / **ma mère** (my mother — feminine)
- BUT: **mon amie** — "my girlfriend/female friend." Before a vowel, *ma* becomes *mon* for sound reasons. The grammar serves the mouth.

*Screen 8 — Family as Social Code (narrative)*
In France, family is private until offered. You do not ask a new acquaintance about their spouse, parents, or children at a first meeting — this is considered intrusive. The French compartmentalize: colleagues see one version of you; family sees another. The question *Vous êtes marié(e)?* (Are you married?) from a stranger crosses a boundary. Wait to be invited into someone's family story. When the invitation comes, it means something.

*Screen 9 — Surnames and History (cultural slide)*
French family names (*noms de famille*) often reveal origin. *Martin* — from the name of the patron saint of France, St. Martin of Tours. *Dupont* — *du pont* (of the bridge) — an ancestor who lived near one. *Leblanc* — the white-haired or pale one. *Charpentier* — carpenter. *Boucher* — butcher. French surnames were mostly standardized in the 14th-15th centuries during tax registration — the state needing to track families is when most people got permanent surnames.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- Core family (père, mère, frère, sœur, fils, fille) are direct descendants of Latin legal vocabulary.
- *Beau/belle* prefix means both step- and in-law. Context decides.
- Possessives match the noun, not the owner. *Mon* before vowels regardless of gender.
- French family is private. Don't ask; wait to be told.

**Grammar Point:** The Verb *Avoir* (to have) — Présent
*J'ai, tu as, il/elle a, nous avons, vous avez, ils/elles ont.*
French uses *avoir* to describe possession AND age: *J'ai vingt ans* (I have twenty years) — not "I am twenty." Age is something you have, not something you are. This is a philosophical stance embedded in grammar.

**Teacher's Note:**
In French, you never say *Je suis vingt ans* (I am twenty). You say *J'ai vingt ans* (I have twenty). Age is a possession. You also *have* hunger (*j'ai faim*), cold (*j'ai froid*), and fear (*j'ai peur*) — all things that in English you *are* or *feel*. French turns states of being into things you carry. Keep this.

## Lesson 5 — Le Temps Qui Passe: Days, Time and the French Relationship with the Calendar

**Level:** A1 | **Theme:** Days of the Week, Time, Months, Seasons
**Central Insight:** French days are named after Roman gods. The week is a map of the ancient sky.

**Cultural Thread:**
Look at the French days of the week and you are looking at the Roman pantheon: *lundi* (Moon), *mardi* (Mars), *mercredi* (Mercury), *jeudi* (Jupiter), *vendredi* (Venus). Only *samedi* (from Hebrew *Shabbat* via Latin) and *dimanche* (from Latin *dies Dominicus*, the Lord's Day) broke the pagan pattern — the intrusion of Judeo-Christian time into the Roman week. This linguistic archaeology is everywhere in French. The calendar is a fossil record of civilizations laid one on top of another.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Le Temps — Time Has a History*
"French days of the week are named after gods who died two thousand years ago. Every time you say *mercredi*, you are invoking Mercury, the winged messenger of Rome. Language is memory."

*Screen 2 — The Days (vocabulary + cultural note)*
- **lundi** — Monday, from *luna* (Moon)
- **mardi** — Tuesday, from *Mars* (god of war)
- **mercredi** — Wednesday, from *Mercure* (Mercury, messenger god)
- **jeudi** — Thursday, from *Jupiter* (king of gods)
- **vendredi** — Friday, from *Vénus* (goddess of love)
- **samedi** — Saturday, from Hebrew *Shabbat* (day of rest)
- **dimanche** — Sunday, from Latin *dies Dominicus* (the Lord's Day)

*Screen 3 — The Week Structure (concept)*
In French calendars, the week begins on **Monday** (*lundi*), not Sunday. This is the European standard and reflects a secular, work-oriented view of time — the weekend is at the *end*, not the beginning. Sunday as the starting day is an American convention inherited from Protestant tradition. The French week says: the week belongs to work and society; the weekend is a reward that follows.

*Screen 4 — Telling Time (concept)*
Formula: **Il est + [number] + heure(s)**
- **Il est deux heures.** — It is 2 o'clock.
- **Il est deux heures et demie.** — It's 2:30 (*et demie* = and a half).
- **Il est deux heures et quart.** — It's 2:15 (*et quart* = and a quarter).
- **Il est deux heures moins le quart.** — It's 1:45 (two o'clock minus a quarter).
Note: France uses 24-hour time in official and written contexts. In speech, context clarifies: *deux heures* could be 2am or 2pm. *Deux heures de l'après-midi* if you need to specify.

*Screen 5 — The Months (vocabulary)*
**janvier, février, mars, avril, mai, juin, juillet, août, septembre, octobre, novembre, décembre**
All from Latin names: *januarius* (Janus, god of doorways), *februarius* (purification month), *martius* (Mars again), etc. Note: *août* (August, from *Augustus Caesar*) — the *ou* combination creates a single sound: "oo." The months are lowercase in French. The language makes no monuments of time.

*Screen 6 — The Seasons (cards2)*
Left: **le printemps** (spring), **l'été** (summer)
Right: **l'automne** (autumn), **l'hiver** (winter)
Note: *l'automne* — the silent letters are typical of French's preservation of Latin endings. *Autumnus* in Latin → the -us dropped; the -n remains silent but shapes the nasal vowel before it. You hear it in the vowel even though you don't pronounce it.

*Screen 7 — Time Expressions (vocabulary)*
- **aujourd'hui** — today (*au* + *jour* + *d'hui*, a redundant stack: "on the day of this day")
- **hier** — yesterday (from Latin *heri*)
- **demain** — tomorrow (from Latin *de mane*, from the morning)
- **la semaine dernière / prochaine** — last week / next week
- **maintenant** — now (from Latin *manu tenere*, to hold in hand — "what you hold right now")

*Screen 8 — Dates (concept)*
French date format: **le + cardinal number + month + year**
*le 14 juillet 1789* — July 14, 1789 (the fall of the Bastille)
Exception: the first of the month uses the ordinal: **le premier mai** (the first of May), not *le un mai*. Only the first. After that, cardinals.

*Screen 9 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Aujourd'hui, c'est mercredi.** — Today is Wednesday.
- **Il est midi et demi.** — It's half past noon.
- **Mon anniversaire est le vingt-deux mars.** — My birthday is March 22.
- **À quelle heure est le train ?** — What time is the train?
- **Il part à dix-sept heures trente.** — It leaves at 17:30.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- Days named after Roman gods + two religious exceptions.
- French week starts Monday.
- Time: *Il est X heure(s)*, with *et demie*, *et quart*, *moins le quart*.
- Months lowercase; first of month uses ordinal; rest use cardinals.
- *Aujourd'hui* is etymologically a tautology — "the day of this day" — and no one cares.

**Grammar Point:** The Verb *Être* (to be) — Présent
*Je suis, tu es, il/elle est, nous sommes, vous êtes, ils/elles sont.*
The circumflex (^) in *êtes* is a memorial: it marks a lost *s* from Old French *estes*. French accents are often gravestones for sounds that disappeared. The history is in the marks.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Maintenant* — "now" — comes from Latin *manu tenere*: to hold in hand. The present moment was, for the Romans, something you physically gripped. The French word for "now" is literally "holding in the hand." Use it knowing this.

## Lesson 6 — Le Corps: The Body, Pain, and Medical French

**Level:** A1 | **Theme:** The Body
**Central Insight:** French medical vocabulary is among the most Latin-saturated in everyday speech — because French medicine never stopped being Latin.

**Cultural Thread:**
French medicine retained Latin as its professional language far longer than other Western traditions. When the first great French medical schools opened in Montpellier (founded 1220) and Paris, instruction was in Latin. The French equivalent of "I have a headache" — *j'ai mal à la tête* — uses *mal* from Latin *malum* (evil, bad thing), which is also the root of *malade* (sick), *malaise* (unease), and *malheur* (misfortune, literally "bad hour"). To be ill in French is to have an evil thing within you. The body vocabulary is a history of how French people have understood suffering.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Le Corps — The Architecture of the Self*
"The French word for sick is *malade*. The word for misfortune is *malheur*. Both come from the Latin *malum*: evil. The language says: suffering is not neutral. It is the intrusion of something wrong into something that should be right."

*Screen 2 — The Head (vocabulary + illustration)*
- **la tête** — head (from Latin *testa*, clay pot — the skull as vessel)
- **les yeux** (pl.) / **l'œil** (sing.) — eyes (the plural is completely irregular — from Latin *oculi* vs. *oculus*)
- **le nez** — nose (Latin *nasus*)
- **la bouche** — mouth (Latin *bucca*, cheek)
- **les oreilles** — ears (Latin *auricula*, little ear)
- **les dents** — teeth (Latin *dentes*)
- **les cheveux** — hair (on head only — Latin *capilli*)

*Screen 3 — The Torso (vocabulary)*
- **le cou** — neck
- **les épaules** — shoulders
- **la poitrine** — chest/breast
- **le dos** — back
- **le ventre** — belly/stomach (not *l'estomac*, which is the organ — *le ventre* is the exterior abdomen)
- **le cœur** — heart (from Latin *cor* — also in: *courage*, *accord*, *cordial*)

*Screen 4 — The Limbs (vocabulary)*
- **les bras** (m.) — arms (plural same as singular — no added *s* because *bras* already ends in *s*)
- **les mains** — hands
- **les doigts** — fingers
- **les jambes** — legs
- **les genoux** — knees (note: irregular plural, *-ou* → *-oux*)
- **les pieds** — feet

*Screen 5 — Pain (concept)*
The French expression for pain is *avoir mal à* — to have evil/pain in.
Formula: **J'ai mal + au/à la/aux + [body part]**
- **J'ai mal à la tête.** — I have a headache. (feminine: *la tête* → *à la*)
- **J'ai mal au dos.** — My back hurts. (masculine: *le dos* → *au*)
- **J'ai mal aux dents.** — I have a toothache. (plural: *les dents* → *aux*)
The preposition *à* contracts with the masculine and plural articles: *à + le = au*, *à + les = aux*.

*Screen 6 — At the Doctor (examples)*
- **Je ne me sens pas bien.** — I don't feel well. (*se sentir* — to feel oneself)
- **J'ai de la fièvre.** — I have a fever. (note partitive: *de la* fever)
- **Depuis quand ?** — Since when? (what the doctor asks)
- **Depuis hier soir.** — Since last night.
- **Vous avez une ordonnance ?** — Do you have a prescription?

*Screen 7 — The Pharmacy (cultural slide)*
France has one of the densest networks of pharmacies (*pharmacies*) in the world — legally mandated to display a green cross. The French pharmacist (*le pharmacien*) is a full medical professional and the first stop for minor ailments. Asking *Qu'est-ce que vous recommandez pour...?* (What do you recommend for...?) is entirely normal and gets genuine medical advice. The French relationship with medication is famous: France is one of the world's highest per-capita consumers of pharmaceuticals. The hypochondria, if that's what it is, is culturally embedded.

*Screen 8 — Body as Emotional Language (concept)*
French uses the body for emotional expressions:
- **Avoir le cœur brisé** — to have a broken heart
- **Avoir les pieds sur terre** — to have feet on the ground (to be grounded/practical)
- **Avoir du cran** — to have guts (*cran* = notch, implying backbone)
- **Avoir le ventre plein** — to have a full belly (to be satisfied)
The body in French is also a metaphor machine.

*Screen 9 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Montrez-moi où vous avez mal.** — Show me where it hurts.
- **J'ai mal à la gorge depuis trois jours.** — I've had a sore throat for three days.
- **Il faut aller chez le médecin.** — You need to go to the doctor.
- **Prenez ce médicament trois fois par jour.** — Take this medicine three times a day.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- Body vocabulary is heavily Latinate — *cœur*, *tête*, *dents* all from direct Latin roots.
- Pain formula: *avoir mal à* + article + body part.
- *À + le = au*, *à + les = aux* — these contractions are mandatory.
- The pharmacy is a medical institution. The pharmacist is a professional.

**Grammar Point:** Contracted Articles — *au* and *aux*
*À + le → au. À + les → aux. De + le → du. De + les → des.*
These contractions are not optional. You cannot say *à le* in French — it does not exist. The language physically forbids the combination and forces the contraction. This is French grammar's insistence on economy.

**Teacher's Note:**
French people say *j'ai mal au ventre* (stomach ache) far more often than they say *j'ai mal à l'estomac*. *Le ventre* (belly) is the popular word; *l'estomac* is clinical. The same distinction runs through the whole medical register: there's always a Latin clinical term and a vernacular term, and knowing which is appropriate marks your register.

## Lesson 7 — Chez Soi: The Home and the Sacred Space

**Level:** A1 | **Theme:** Home & Living
**Central Insight:** The French *chez* has no English equivalent — it embodies a philosophy of belonging.

**Cultural Thread:**
The word *chez* — as in *chez moi* (at my place), *chez vous* (at your place), *chez Dupont* (at Dupont's) — comes from the Old French *chiese*, from Latin *casa* (cottage, house). But it has evolved far beyond its etymology into something untranslatable. *Chez* means the inhabited space of a person — not just the building, but the world they have made of it. *Allons chez Marie* is "let's go to Marie's" — but the phrase implies more: Marie's world, Marie's kitchen, the version of her that exists in her own domain. The Moroccan-French philosopher Jacques Derrida wrote about *chez soi* as the fundamental condition of having a self: to exist is to have a *chez*.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Chez Soi — A Word for Belonging*
"French has a word that means 'the space someone has made their own.' You cannot translate it. You can only learn it. It's *chez*."

*Screen 2 — The House (vocabulary)*
- **la maison** — house (from Latin *mansio*, a place to stay — also the root of English "mansion")
- **l'appartement** — apartment
- **l'immeuble** — apartment building
- **l'étage** — floor/storey (*au premier étage* = on the second floor in French; the ground floor is *le rez-de-chaussée*)
- **la cave** — cellar (*une cave à vins* = a wine cellar)
- **le grenier** — attic (*grenier* also means granary — historically the same space)

*Screen 3 — Rooms (vocabulary)*
- **la cuisine** — kitchen (and also cooking — the same word)
- **le salon** / **le séjour** — living room (the first is the formal reception space; the second is more everyday)
- **la chambre** — bedroom (*chambre* means any private room; historically: the private room of the king was *la chambre du roi*)
- **la salle de bain** — bathroom (literally "the hall of the bath")
- **les toilettes** — toilet/WC (always plural in French, never *la toilette* for the room)
- **l'entrée** / **le couloir** — entrance hall / corridor

*Screen 4 — Furniture Essentials (vocabulary)*
- **la table** — table
- **la chaise** — chair (from Old French *chaire*, from Latin *cathedra* — a bishop's throne; *chaise* diverged from *chaire* in the 17th century)
- **le lit** — bed
- **l'armoire** — wardrobe (an *armoire* stores weapons in medieval usage; now it stores shirts)
- **le canapé** — sofa (from Arabic *qanafa* through Turkish — a word that traveled from the Middle East to French living rooms)

*Screen 5 — Chez: The Untranslatable (concept)*
Formula: **chez + [person/pronoun]** = at someone's home/domain
- **chez moi** — at my place
- **chez toi** — at your place
- **chez lui / elle** — at his/her place
- **chez nous** — at our place / (culturally) in our world
- **chez le médecin** — at the doctor's (their professional space is their *chez*)
- **chez Balzac** — in Balzac's work (a literary extension: the world the author made)

*Screen 6 — Invitations and the French Home (narrative)*
Being invited into a French home is significant. Unlike in some cultures where the home is a casual open space, the French home is private — you are shown the salon, perhaps the kitchen; the bedrooms remain closed. The threshold matters. When you cross it, you are entering someone's *chez soi*. This is why the secret lesson on dinner invitations exists: the protocols surrounding entry into the French home are dense, specific, and carry weight that outsiders consistently underestimate.

*Screen 7 — Location Phrases (concept)*
- **dans la cuisine** — in the kitchen
- **sur la table** — on the table
- **sous le lit** — under the bed
- **devant la maison** — in front of the house
- **derrière l'armoire** — behind the wardrobe
- **entre la chaise et le mur** — between the chair and the wall

*Screen 8 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Je cherche un appartement dans le 11e arrondissement.** — I'm looking for an apartment in the 11th arrondissement.
- **Mon bureau est au premier étage.** — My office is on the second floor.
- **Chez nous, on mange toujours ensemble.** — At our house, we always eat together.
- **Il y a deux chambres et un grand salon.** — There are two bedrooms and a large living room.
- **La salle de bain est au bout du couloir.** — The bathroom is at the end of the corridor.

*Screen 9 — Il y a (concept)*
**Il y a** = there is / there are
Literally: *il* (it) + *y* (there) + *a* (has, from *avoir*). "It has there." A construction that defies word-for-word translation but is completely intuitive in use. **Il y a des fleurs sur la table.** — There are flowers on the table.
**Il n'y a pas de** — there is/are no [negation]. Note: after negation, the article disappears: *des fleurs* → *pas de fleurs*.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- *Chez* is one of French's most essential untranslatables — domain, belonging, the inhabited self.
- French floors: ground floor = *rez-de-chaussée*; first floor = *premier étage* (= second floor in American).
- *Il y a* for existence of things; negation drops the article.
- The French home is private. Invitation means something.

**Grammar Point:** Prepositions of Place
*Dans* (in), *sur* (on), *sous* (under), *devant* (in front of), *derrière* (behind), *entre* (between), *près de* (near), *loin de* (far from). These do not change. Unlike verbs or articles, prepositions in French are invariable. They are the skeleton of sentences.

**Teacher's Note:**
The French say *chez le boulanger* (at the baker's) and *chez le médecin* (at the doctor's) — the professional space is treated as the person's home. This extends even to authors: *chez Proust, le temps est une obsession* (in Proust's work, time is an obsession). *Chez* is so powerful it can enter a corpus of literature.

## Lesson 8 — Les Couleurs et les Adjectifs: How French Describes the World

**Level:** A1 | **Theme:** Colours & Adjectives
**Central Insight:** French adjectives follow the noun they describe — the thing comes first, its quality second. This is not arbitrary; it is a philosophical stance on description.

**Cultural Thread:**
In English, you say "the red house." In French: *la maison rouge* — the house red. The noun arrives before its modifier. French linguists call this the "noun-centric" structure: the object exists first, and description is added as an afterthought, a refinement. There are exceptions — adjectives of beauty, age, goodness, and size (*beau, vieux, bon, grand*) come before — but these exceptions reveal the deeper rule: only the most subjective, evaluative qualities go before the noun. Everything objective (colour, shape, origin, material) follows. French grammar enforces epistemology.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Le Monde en Couleurs — The World in Colours*
"In French, the house is red. Not the red house. The thing arrives before its quality. Once you know this, you hear French differently."

*Screen 2 — Colours (vocabulary)*
- **rouge** — red
- **bleu/bleue** — blue (two forms: masculine/feminine)
- **vert/verte** — green
- **jaune** — yellow (invariable — same for masc./fem.)
- **blanc/blanche** — white (the feminine changes significantly)
- **noir/noire** — black
- **gris/grise** — grey
- **orange** — orange (borrowed from Persian via Arabic via Portuguese — invariable, never changes for gender or number)
- **rose** — pink (the word for rose flower became the word for pink)

*Screen 3 — Adjective Agreement (concept)*
Formula: Adjective must agree in gender and number with the noun.
- *un livre rouge* — a red book (masc. sing.)
- *une robe rouge* — a red dress (fem. sing.) — *rouge* doesn't change because it ends in *e* already
- *des livres rouges* — red books (plural, adds *s*)
- *une voiture verte* — a green car (fem.: *vert* → *verte*)
The feminine of most adjectives adds a silent *e*, which often makes the final consonant audible: *petit/petite* (puh-tee/puh-teet) — the *t* sounds in the feminine.

*Screen 4 — Descriptive Adjectives (vocabulary)*
- **grand/grande** — big/tall (before noun: exceptional status)
- **petit/petite** — small
- **beau/belle** — beautiful (before noun: irregular in masculine singular *beau* → *bel* before vowel)
- **vieux/vieille** — old (very irregular: *vieux/vieil/vieille*)
- **nouveau/nouvelle** — new
- **bon/bonne** — good (doubles the final consonant in feminine)

*Screen 5 — The BANGS Rule (concept)*
Adjectives that go BEFORE the noun: **Beauty, Age, Number, Goodness, Size**
- *un beau tableau* — a beautiful painting (beauty)
- *une vieille ville* — an old town (age)
- *le premier ministre* — the first minister (number/order)
- *une bonne idée* — a good idea (goodness)
- *une grande fenêtre* — a large window (size)
Everything else follows the noun. BANGS is the exception that proves the rule.

*Screen 6 — Adjective Position Changes Meaning (concept)*
Some adjectives change meaning depending on position:
- **un homme grand** — a tall man (physical)
- **un grand homme** — a great man (historical significance)
- **ma chère amie** — my dear friend (before: emotional)
- **une robe chère** — an expensive dress (after: financial)
- **un certain problème** — a particular/specific problem (before)
- **une réponse certaine** — a certain/definite answer (after)
This ambiguity is a feature. French precision requires the speaker to choose placement carefully.

*Screen 7 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Elle a les yeux bleus et les cheveux noirs.** — She has blue eyes and black hair.
- **C'est une belle journée.** — It's a beautiful day.
- **Je cherche un appartement petit et pas cher.** — I'm looking for a small and inexpensive apartment.
- **Il porte un manteau gris et une écharpe rouge.** — He's wearing a grey coat and a red scarf.

*Screen 8 — Colour in French Culture (narrative)*
The colour *bleu* is embedded in French identity in ways that run deeper than the flag. *Bleu de France* has been the royal colour since the Capetian dynasty (987 AD). *Les Bleus* (the French national football team) are named for it. The French passport is *bleu nuit* (midnight blue). But French also has a melancholy relationship with blue: *avoir les bleus* (to have the blues) exists in French too — borrowed from English jazz, but the French kept it because it resonated with something they recognized.

*Screen 9 — Basic Colour Idioms (vocabulary)*
- **voir la vie en rose** — to see life in pink (to be optimistic — popularized by Édith Piaf)
- **avoir carte blanche** — to have white card (to have full freedom to act)
- **rire jaune** — to laugh yellow (to give a forced, uncomfortable laugh)
- **broyer du noir** — to grind black (to be deeply depressed, to brood)

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- Adjectives go after the noun in most cases. BANGS adjectives go before.
- Adjectives agree in gender and number: *-e* for feminine, *-s* for plural.
- Some adjectives change meaning by position.
- *Orange* and other borrowed colour words are invariable.

**Grammar Point:** Adjective Agreement
Masculine: base form. Feminine: add *-e* (unless already ends in *e*). Plural: add *-s*. Double feminine: *bon/bonne, ancien/ancienne, gros/grosse* (double the final consonant before adding *-e*). Irregular: *blanc/blanche, vieux/vieille, beau/belle, nouveau/nouvelle*.

**Teacher's Note:**
When French says *une grande dame* (a great lady — before), it means stature, dignity, cultural weight. When it says *une dame grande* (a tall lady — after), it means physical height. This positional distinction is something French speakers do intuitively; foreigners learn it consciously. But the effort of consciousness makes you understand the language more deeply than a native speaker who never had to think about it.

## Lesson 9 — Les Verbes Essentiels: Être, Avoir, Aller, Faire

**Level:** A1 | **Theme:** Core Verbs
**Central Insight:** Four verbs — to be, to have, to go, to do — are the load-bearing pillars of all French speech. Master these four and you can build everything.

**Cultural Thread:**
In French grammatical tradition, *être* and *avoir* are called *les verbes auxiliaires* — the auxiliary verbs, the helpers. They support every other tense. Every event in French that happened in the past is expressed through *être* or *avoir* as an auxiliary — which means you cannot narrate a single past event without using them. The choice of which to use (être for movement/state; avoir for most everything else) is itself a statement about the nature of the verb. French grammar forces you to categorize every action as either one that affects position/being, or one that affects the world. It is an inadvertent classification of all human experience.

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Les Piliers — The Load-Bearing Verbs*
"Four verbs hold up the French language. Lose one and you lose your ability to speak. Learn them and you hold the architecture."

*Screen 2 — Être: To Be (concept + table)*
**je suis** — I am
**tu es** — you are
**il / elle est** — he / she is
**nous sommes** — we are
**vous êtes** — you are (formal/plural)
**ils / elles sont** — they are
Uses: identity, profession, origin, description, location (with certain verbs).
*Je suis étudiant(e). Il est français. Nous sommes à Paris.*

*Screen 3 — Avoir: To Have (concept + table)*
**j'ai** — I have
**tu as** — you have
**il / elle a** — he / she has
**nous avons** — we have
**vous avez** — you have
**ils / elles ont** — they have
Uses: possession, age, physical states (*faim, soif, froid, chaud, peur*).
*J'ai vingt ans. Tu as faim ? Elle a une voiture.*

*Screen 4 — Être vs. Avoir in Expressions (compare)*
Left — **ÊTRE** (states of being):
- *Je suis fatigué.* — I am tired. (describing the state)
- *Il est malade.* — He is sick.
- *Nous sommes en retard.* — We are late.

Right — **AVOIR** (physical sensations + age):
- *J'ai faim.* — I am hungry. (literally: I have hunger)
- *Tu as froid.* — You are cold.
- *Elle a trente ans.* — She is thirty.
The distinction: sustained states use *être*; temporary physical sensations as nouns use *avoir*.

*Screen 5 — Aller: To Go (concept + table)*
**je vais** — I go / I am going
**tu vas** — you go
**il / elle va** — he/she goes
**nous allons** — we go
**vous allez** — you go
**ils / elles vont** — they go
Uses: movement AND the immediate future.
*Je vais à Paris. Elle va bien. Nous allons manger.* (We are going to eat.)

*Screen 6 — Aller as Future (concept)*
Formula: **aller + infinitive** = immediate future
- *Je vais manger.* — I'm going to eat.
- *Il va pleuvoir.* — It's going to rain.
- *Nous allons partir demain.* — We're going to leave tomorrow.
This construction — so similar to English "going to" — is the most common way to express the near future in spoken French. The compound tenses (*futur simple*) exist but are felt as more formal, more written. *Aller + infinitif* is how French people actually talk about what's about to happen.

*Screen 7 — Faire: To Do / To Make (concept + table)*
**je fais** — I do / I make
**tu fais** — you do
**il / elle fait** — he/she does
**nous faisons** — we do (note: *fai-sɔ̃*, not *fai-zɔ̃* — a pronunciation trap)
**vous faites** — you do (completely irregular)
**ils / elles font** — they do
Uses: actions, weather, sports, housework.
*Il fait beau. Je fais du sport. Qu'est-ce que tu fais ?*

*Screen 8 — Faire in Key Expressions (vocabulary)*
- **faire du sport** — to do sports
- **faire la cuisine** — to cook
- **faire les courses** — to do the shopping
- **faire la queue** — to queue / stand in line
- **faire un tour** — to take a walk / a trip
- **faire attention** — to pay attention
- **ça fait combien ?** — how much does it come to?

*Screen 9 — Real Sentences (examples)*
- **Qu'est-ce que vous faites dans la vie ?** — What do you do for a living?
- **Je suis professeur et j'ai trente-cinq ans.** — I'm a teacher and I'm thirty-five.
- **Elle va chez le médecin demain.** — She's going to the doctor tomorrow.
- **Nous allons faire un tour dans Paris.** — We're going to take a walk around Paris.
- **Il fait froid aujourd'hui — je vais rester chez moi.** — It's cold today — I'm going to stay home.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- *Être*: states, identity, description.
- *Avoir*: possession, age, physical sensations.
- *Aller*: movement + immediate future with infinitive.
- *Faire*: actions, weather, sports, housework.
- All four are highly irregular. There is no shortcut. Frequency makes them automatic.

**Grammar Point:** The Infinitive and Verb Groups
French verbs end in *-er* (the largest group, mostly regular), *-ir*, or *-re*. The infinitive is the dictionary form: *parler, finir, prendre*. But *être, avoir, aller, faire* are outside these groups — they are so ancient and so commonly used that they never regularized. Irregular means important.

**Teacher's Note:**
*Faire du sport* — doing sport — uses the partitive *du*. You do "some sport," not "the sport." This is because sports were originally treated like food: an activity you consumed a portion of. *Faire du piano, faire de la natation, faire du vélo* — all use this structure. When you say *je fais de la natation*, you are participating in a tradition of language that treats leisure activities as consumable substance.

## Lesson 10 — Le Genre et les Articles: The Great Sorting Machine

**Level:** A1 | **Theme:** Gender & Articles
**Central Insight:** French grammar divides all objects in the world into two categories: masculine and feminine. This is not about sex. It is a filing system inherited from Latin, and its logic is mostly historical.

**Cultural Thread:**
The question every French learner asks within the first week: *why is* la lune *feminine and* le soleil *masculine? In German, the moon is masculine and the sun is feminine. In Italian, they match the French. There is no universal logic — there is only the history of Latin grammatical gender, which assigned categories based on a now-lost set of rules that no longer operate. The French Académie française has been trying to stabilize gender for centuries. In 1994, they officially ruled that certain job titles must have feminine forms (*une médecin, une auteure, une chercheuse*) — and then other rulings contradicted these. French gender is a living debate, not a settled system. Entering into it is entering into the intellectual life of the language.*

**Teaching Arc:**

*Screen 1 — Opening (cover)*
Title: *Le Genre — The Sorting Machine*
"French puts every noun into one of two boxes: masculine or feminine. The moon is feminine. The sun is masculine. A dress is feminine. A skirt is masculine. This is not logic. This is history. And history, once it sets, is very hard to change."

*Screen 2 — The Definite Article (concept)*
**le** — the (masculine singular): *le livre*, *le train*, *le soleil*
**la** — the (feminine singular): *la lune*, *la table*, *la maison*
**l'** — the (before vowel or mute *h*): *l'arbre*, *l'heure*, *l'homme*
**les** — the (plural, all genders): *les livres*, *les maisons*
Rule: *le/la* → *l'* before a vowel. This is elision — the language drops the vowel to avoid the awkward collision of two vowel sounds.

*Screen 3 — The Indefinite Article (concept)*
**un** — a/an (masculine): *un livre*, *un café*
**une** — a/an (feminine): *une table*, *une idée*
**des** — some (plural): *des livres*, *des idées*
Note: *un* and *une* are also the numbers 1 (masculine) and 1 (feminine). The indefinite article and the number are the same word. This is not coincidence — both come from Latin *unum*.

*Screen 4 — The Partitive Article (concept)*
**du** = *de* + *le* — some (masculine uncountable)
**de la** — some (feminine uncountable)
**de l'** — some (before vowel)
**des** — some (plural countable)
Used for: quantities you can't count — food, liquids, abstract concepts.
*Je bois du café. Elle mange de la soupe. Il a de la patience.*
After negation: all become **de** (or **d'** before vowel): *Je ne bois pas de café.*

*Screen 5 — Gender Patterns (concept)*
There is no perfect rule, but patterns help:
**Usually masculine:** nouns ending in *-age* (le garage, le message), *-ment* (le gouvernement, le sentiment), *-isme* (le capitalisme), *-eau* (le bateau, le chapeau)
**Usually feminine:** nouns ending in *-tion* (la nation), *-tion* (la situation), *-té* (la liberté), *-ure* (la nature), *-sse* (la tristesse), *-ance* (la confiance)
These are tendencies, not laws. Exception: *le lycée*, *le musée* (end in *-ée*, masculine). *La plume* (end in *-e*, feminine). The patterns hold about 80% of the time.

*Screen 6 — False Logic: Don't Trust Your Intuition (concept)*
Common gender traps:
- **un problème** — masculine (though it sounds like it could be feminine)
- **une image** — feminine (though *-age* endings are usually masculine)
- **le silence** — masculine
- **la page** — feminine
- **le livre** (book) masculine; **la livre** (pound sterling, pound of weight) feminine — same word, different gender, different meaning.

*Screen 7 — Gender of People and Jobs (concept)*
Traditional: French uses masculine as the default for mixed groups.
*Les étudiants sont arrivés.* — could mean all men, or a mixed group.
Modern evolution: *Les étudiant(e)s* (written) or *les étudiant·e·s* (inclusive writing). Spoken language is adapting slowly. The Académie française issued a ruling against *écriture inclusive* in 2021; teachers' unions largely ignore it. Gender in language is politically alive in France in a way that reaches from grammar classrooms to the Senate.

*Screen 8 — Articles in Practice (examples)*
- **Le président parle à la nation.** — The president speaks to the nation.
- **J'achète une baguette et du beurre.** — I'm buying a baguette and some butter.
- **Est-ce qu'il y a des places disponibles ?** — Are there seats available?
- **Je n'ai pas de voiture.** — I don't have a car.

*Screen 9 — Memorizing Gender (narrative)*
The only reliable strategy: **learn every noun with its article**. Not *soleil* — but *le soleil*. Not *lune* — but *la lune*. The article is part of the word. Professional language learners know this; most beginners learn it the hard way. From lesson one, every new noun should enter your memory with its gender attached.

*Screen 10 — Summary*
- All French nouns are masculine or feminine. No neutral.
- *le/la/l'/les* for definite. *un/une/des* for indefinite. *du/de la/de l'/des* for partitive.
- After negation, all become *de/d'*.
- Learn every noun with its article. No exceptions to this rule.
- Gender is historical, not logical — but patterns (about 80% reliable) help.

**Grammar Point:** The Definite, Indefinite, and Partitive Articles — Complete Summary
See Screen 2–4 above. The key exam insight: *Je veux un café* (a coffee, one coffee) vs. *Je veux du café* (some coffee, an unspecified amount). The choice between indefinite and partitive is a choice about whether the noun is countable.

**Teacher's Note:**
The word *la mer* (the sea) is feminine; *le lac* (the lake) is masculine. *La forêt* is feminine; *le bois* is masculine. There may be some ancient pattern in what the Gauls assigned as feminine or masculine — sky-things feminine, earth-things masculine? It's not consistent enough to be a rule. But the speculation reveals something important: gender in language is not biology — it is a filing system that once had a logic that has now dissolved. You're learning the fossilized remains of a categorization scheme whose architect is unknown.
