# The Expression Calendar — 365 Days of French Wit, Poetry, and Obsession

> *"Bien écrire, c'est tout à la fois bien penser, bien sentir et bien rendre."*
> — Buffon

The daily expression is not a bonus. It is the daily pulse of the French module — the reminder that language is alive, that it has a history, and that some of its best moments are four to eight words that contain a civilization.

Each expression has: the phrase in French, its literal word-for-word translation (often where the magic is), its actual meaning in use, an example sentence, its origin, and a theme. Organized in 8 thematic groups, drawn from France's four great obsessions (food, bureaucracy, love, death) plus wit, time, philosophy, and the natural world.

---

## FOOD (La Table) — 12 expressions

---

**1. Avoir d'autres chats à fouetter**
*Literal:* To have other cats to whip
*Meaning:* To have other things to deal with; to have bigger fish to fry
*Example:* *"Tu vas t'occuper de ça maintenant ?" — "Non, j'ai d'autres chats à fouetter."*
*Origin:* 17th century. When public punishments included whipping, the *fouetteur* (flogger) was a busy professional. But this expression references a practice from street spectacle: performing animals, including cats that were "whipped" (startled into tricks). The image survives long after the practice is forgotten.
*Theme:* Pragmatism

---

**2. Mettre de l'eau dans son vin**
*Literal:* To put water in one's wine
*Meaning:* To moderate one's position; to climb down; to be less extreme
*Example:* *"Au début il refusait tout compromis, mais il a fini par mettre de l'eau dans son vin."*
*Origin:* Ancient. The Greeks and Romans regularly diluted wine with water — undiluted wine was considered barbaric. By the 17th century, the metaphor had inverted: adding water to wine meant diluting the strength of a position.
*Theme:* Compromise

---

**3. Avoir le beurre et l'argent du beurre**
*Literal:* To have the butter and the butter money
*Meaning:* To want it both ways; to have your cake and eat it
*Example:* *"Il voulait partir en vacances mais garder tous ses avantages — on ne peut pas avoir le beurre et l'argent du beurre."*
*Origin:* A market expression from rural France: you could sell your butter (and get the money) or keep your butter (and eat it), but once sold, the butter is gone. The French market logic applied to greed.
*Theme:* Greed / Pragmatism

---

**4. Tourner autour du pot**
*Literal:* To turn around the pot
*Meaning:* To beat around the bush; to avoid the point
*Example:* *"Arrête de tourner autour du pot et dis-moi ce que tu penses vraiment."*
*Origin:* Medieval kitchen: a servant who kept circling the pot without actually serving from it — delaying, deferring, never getting to the point. The kitchen as the origin of social metaphor.
*Theme:* Communication

---

**5. Les carottes sont cuites**
*Literal:* The carrots are cooked
*Meaning:* It's over; it's too late to change anything; the die is cast
*Example:* *"Ils ont signé le contrat ce matin — les carottes sont cuites."*
*Origin:* Once carrots are cooked, you cannot uncook them. The irreversibility of cooking as a metaphor for the irreversibility of certain situations. Used to express resignation, sometimes with dark humor.
*Theme:* Resignation / Fate

---

**6. Avoir le cafard**
*Literal:* To have the cockroach
*Meaning:* To feel down; to have the blues
*Example:* *"J'ai le cafard depuis trois jours — la pluie n'arrange rien."*
*Origin:* See Badge Lore (B1 Lesson 9). Baudelairean tradition. The cockroach as symbol of the dark underside.
*Theme:* Emotion / Food (this cockroach lives in kitchens)

---

**7. Couper la poire en deux**
*Literal:* To cut the pear in two
*Meaning:* To split the difference; to compromise equally
*Example:* *"On ne s'accordait pas sur le prix. On a coupé la poire en deux à 150 euros."*
*Origin:* The image of a pear (not an apple — more fragile, more delicate, sweeter) split exactly in two: a physical image of equal division. The pear as the fruit of compromise.
*Theme:* Compromise / Negotiation

---

**8. Boire la tasse**
*Literal:* To drink the cup
*Meaning:* To swallow a mouthful of water while swimming; more broadly: to be overwhelmed; to "go under" financially
*Example:* *"L'entreprise a bu la tasse cette année avec la crise."*
*Origin:* Swimming metaphor — accidentally swallowing water is called *boire la tasse* (drinking the cup). Extended to business failure: swallowing the bitter cup of reversal.
*Theme:* Failure / Endurance

---

**9. En faire tout un fromage**
*Literal:* To make a whole cheese out of it
*Meaning:* To make a mountain out of a molehill; to over-complicate something
*Example:* *"C'est juste un retard de cinq minutes — n'en fais pas tout un fromage."*
*Origin:* Cheesemaking is an elaborate, time-consuming process. To make a whole cheese out of something small is to invest absurd effort in a minor matter.
*Theme:* Proportion / Pragmatism

---

**10. Casser du sucre sur le dos de quelqu'un**
*Literal:* To break sugar on someone's back
*Meaning:* To talk about someone behind their back; to criticize in their absence
*Example:* *"Je sais qu'il casse du sucre sur mon dos au bureau, mais je m'en fiche."*
*Origin:* The sugar-breaking image — the old-fashioned cone of sugar that had to be broken with a special tool — applied to the act of shattering someone's reputation with blows they cannot see coming. The absence of the target makes the criticism feel safer, like breaking something that can't defend itself.
*Theme:* Social Relations / Gossip

---

**11. Avoir le ventre creux**
*Literal:* To have a hollow belly
*Meaning:* To be hungry; to be empty
*Example:* *"On n'a pas pris le temps de déjeuner — on a le ventre creux."*
*Origin:* A direct, physical image of hunger — the hollow belly that needs filling. One of the oldest French expressions of bodily need, without metaphor.
*Theme:* Hunger / The Body

---

**12. Jeter de la poudre aux yeux**
*Literal:* To throw powder in someone's eyes
*Meaning:* To dazzle and deceive; to impress with appearances to mask the reality
*Example:* *"Ces chiffres impressionnants ne sont que de la poudre aux yeux — la réalité est bien plus sombre."*
*Origin:* 17th century. Street conjurers and charlatans would throw powder (flour, chalk, sand) in the eyes of the crowd to create a moment of blindness during which their trick occurred. To dazzle through deception.
*Theme:* Deception / Performance

---

## BUREAUCRACY (Le Guichet) — 12 expressions

---

**13. Se perdre dans les méandres de l'administration**
*Literal:* To get lost in the meanders of administration
*Meaning:* To be caught in bureaucratic complexity
*Example:* *"Je me suis perdu dans les méandres de l'administration pour obtenir mon permis de construire."*
*Origin:* The *Méandre* river (in modern Turkey) gave its name to all winding watercourses. The image of an administration as a winding river — going in circles, doubling back, always arriving somewhere unexpected.
*Theme:* Bureaucracy

---

**14. C'est le serpent qui se mord la queue**
*Literal:* It's the snake biting its own tail
*Meaning:* It's circular reasoning; a vicious cycle; a situation that returns to its own origin
*Example:* *"Pour obtenir le document A, il faut le document B, et pour obtenir le B, il faut le A — c'est le serpent qui se mord la queue."*
*Origin:* The Ouroboros — the ancient symbol of the snake eating its tail, representing eternity and circularity — applied to the peculiar circular logic of administrative requirements.
*Theme:* Bureaucracy / Logic

---

**15. Noyer le poisson**
*Literal:* To drown the fish
*Meaning:* To confuse the issue deliberately; to bamboozle; to obscure the main point with irrelevant details
*Example:* *"Le ministre n'a pas répondu à la question — il a noyé le poisson en parlant d'autre chose pendant dix minutes."*
*Origin:* A fishing technique: when you've hooked a large fish, you tire it by letting it run in the water until it drowns in its own exhaustion. Applied to conversation: wearing someone down with digressions until they forget their original question.
*Theme:* Bureaucracy / Deception

---

**16. C'est la croix et la bannière**
*Literal:* It's the cross and the banner
*Meaning:* It's an enormous effort for a simple result; it requires a disproportionate amount of trouble
*Example:* *"Obtenir un rendez-vous chez ce médecin, c'est la croix et la bannière."*
*Origin:* The processional cross and processional banner were the first two items carried in a Catholic religious procession — the most formal, elaborate beginning to a ceremony. To describe something as "the cross and the banner" is to say it requires a full ceremonial mobilization for what ought to be simple.
*Theme:* Bureaucracy / Effort

---

**17. Avoir son rond de serviette**
*Literal:* To have one's napkin ring
*Meaning:* To have an established position; to have one's regular place
*Example:* *"Il a son rond de serviette dans cette institution depuis trente ans — impossible de le déloger."*
*Origin:* In French households before disposable napkins, each family member had a personalized napkin ring in which their cloth napkin lived between meals — so the same person used the same napkin all week. Having your napkin ring meant having a permanent, recognized place at the family table. Extended to institutional belonging.
*Theme:* Institutions / Belonging

---

**18. Faire de la rétention d'information**
*Literal:* To perform information retention
*Meaning:* To deliberately withhold information; to hold back knowledge
*Example:* *"Le directeur pratique la rétention d'information pour garder le pouvoir sur son équipe."*
*Origin:* A modern corporate and administrative term — the *rétention* is a medical word (urinary retention, etc.) applied to the bureaucratic practice of holding information back. The body that doesn't release what it should.
*Theme:* Bureaucracy / Power

---

**19. Botter en touche**
*Literal:* To kick into touch
*Meaning:* To sidestep a question; to avoid answering; to deflect
*Example:* *"Quand on lui a posé la question sur sa démission, il a botté en touche."*
*Origin:* Rugby vocabulary: kicking the ball out of play into the touchline area (*la touche*) is a defensive move when under pressure. The political and administrative use: kicking a difficult question out of play.
*Theme:* Bureaucracy / Politics

---

**20. Se renvoyer la balle**
*Literal:* To send the ball back to each other
*Meaning:* To pass the buck; to deflect responsibility to each other
*Example:* *"Le ministère et la mairie se renvoient la balle — personne ne prend la responsabilité."*
*Origin:* Ball game imagery — two players hitting the ball back and forth without either taking possession. Applied to the administrative habit of passing responsibility from one institution to another.
*Theme:* Bureaucracy / Responsibility

---

**21. Péter les plombs**
*Literal:* To blow one's fuses
*Meaning:* To lose control; to have a meltdown; to snap
*Example:* *"Face à la troisième demande absurde de la journée, elle a pété les plombs."*
*Origin:* Electrical imagery: the fuses (*les plombs*, the lead fuses in old electrical systems) blow when the current is too strong. To blow one's fuses is to exceed one's tolerance. A very French expression for the very French experience of administrative overload.
*Theme:* Bureaucracy / Emotion

---

**22. Mettre les bâtons dans les roues**
*Literal:* To put sticks in the wheels
*Meaning:* To throw a spanner in the works; to obstruct
*Example:* *"Il fait tout pour mettre des bâtons dans les roues du projet.*"
*Origin:* Medieval technique for slowing a runaway cart: jamming a stick through the wheel spokes. Applied to deliberate obstruction of progress.
*Theme:* Obstruction / Bureaucracy

---

**23. C'est un monde!**
*Literal:* It's a world!
*Meaning:* It's outrageous!; The nerve!; The nerve of some people!
*Example:* *"Il a réclamé un remboursement après avoir mangé la moitié du plat — c'est un monde !"*
*Origin:* The *monde* (world) as the social sphere, with its own norms of behavior. *C'est un monde!* = this behavior is so outrageous it constitutes a world unto itself, beyond the norms of civilized society.
*Theme:* Social Indignation

---

**24. Avoir le bras long**
*Literal:* To have a long arm
*Meaning:* To have connections; to have influence; to be able to reach anywhere
*Example:* *"Il a le bras long — il connaît tout le monde dans cette administration."*
*Origin:* The image of a hand that can reach far — across rooms, across institutions, across social strata. Power visualized as physical reach. The long arm of influence.
*Theme:* Power / Networks

---

## LOVE (Le Cœur) — 12 expressions

---

**25. Avoir le coup de foudre**
*Literal:* To have the thunderbolt
*Meaning:* To fall in love at first sight (literally: to be struck by lightning)
*Example:* *"Il a eu le coup de foudre pour elle dès qu'il l'a vue."*
*Origin:* The *foudre* (thunderbolt, lightning) as the divine weapon of sudden transformation. Jupiter's thunderbolt in Roman mythology transformed those it struck. To fall in love is to be struck without warning — transformed by an external force before you have time to decide.
*Theme:* Love

---

**26. Poser un lapin à quelqu'un**
*Literal:* To place a rabbit on someone
*Meaning:* To stand someone up; to not show up for a meeting
*Example:* *"Elle m'a posé un lapin hier soir — j'ai attendu une heure au restaurant."*
*Origin:* 19th century. *Poser un lapin* originally meant to give a woman money for her favors without receiving them (giving her a *lapin* — something soft, hopeless). Later the meaning shifted: to offer a meeting and then fail to appear — giving something that never materializes.
*Theme:* Love / Social Relations

---

**27. Brûler ses vaisseaux**
*Literal:* To burn one's ships
*Meaning:* To burn one's bridges; to make an irreversible decision; to commit completely
*Example:* *"En quittant son emploi sans filet de sécurité, elle a brûlé ses vaisseaux — il n'y a plus de retour en arrière."*
*Origin:* Attributed to Hernán Cortés (1519) — who reputedly scuttled his ships on arriving in Mexico so that his soldiers could not retreat. The French tradition tells a parallel story of classical antiquity. Burning the ships of retreat means there is only forward.
*Theme:* Love / Commitment / Risk

---

**28. Ne pas avoir froid aux yeux**
*Literal:* Not to have cold in one's eyes
*Meaning:* To be bold; to have nerve; to not shrink from challenges
*Example:* *"Elle a osé demander une augmentation dès sa première semaine — elle n'a pas froid aux yeux."*
*Origin:* The cold eyes of the timid — lowered gaze, the eyes that don't meet the challenge. To not have cold in one's eyes is to maintain the warm gaze of courage.
*Theme:* Courage / Love (in declaring love)

---

**29. Chercher midi à quatorze heures**
*Literal:* To look for noon at two in the afternoon
*Meaning:* To look for a difficulty where there is none; to overcomplicate
*Example:* *"La solution est simple — arrête de chercher midi à quatorze heures."*
*Origin:* If you are looking for noon at two in the afternoon, you are seeking something at the wrong time — something that has already passed. The impossible search for a moment that's already over, or a simplicity that the seeker refuses to accept.
*Theme:* Pragmatism / Love (relationships overthought)

---

**30. Filer le parfait amour**
*Literal:* To spin the perfect love
*Meaning:* To be in the perfect harmony of a couple; to be blissfully in love
*Example:* *"Depuis leur rencontre, ils filent le parfait amour."*
*Origin:* The image of love as a thread being *spun* — *filer* = to spin thread, to draw out a fiber into a continuous thread. Perfect love is love that can be drawn out smoothly, without knots, endlessly.
*Theme:* Love

---

**31. Se regarder en chiens de faïence**
*Literal:* To look at each other like faience dogs
*Meaning:* To stare at each other warily, without speaking; to maintain a hostile stalemate
*Example:* *"Après leur dispute, ils se regardaient en chiens de faïence sans dire un mot."*
*Origin:* Faience (glazed ceramic) dog ornaments were placed symmetrically on either side of a fireplace — facing each other in frozen stares, unable to speak or move. Couples after a fight.
*Theme:* Love / Conflict

---

**32. Battre froid à quelqu'un**
*Literal:* To beat cold to someone
*Meaning:* To give someone the cold shoulder; to be deliberately cold toward someone
*Example:* *"Depuis notre dispute, elle me bat froid et ne répond plus à mes messages."*
*Origin:* To *battre* (beat/strike) cold — the cold that strikes rather than comforts. The deliberate application of chill to a relationship that was warm.
*Theme:* Love / Social Relations

---

**33. Avoir quelqu'un dans la peau**
*Literal:* To have someone in one's skin
*Meaning:* To be infatuated; to be under someone's skin; to be passionately attached
*Example:* *"Il l'a dans la peau depuis la première rencontre — il ne pense qu'à elle."*
*Origin:* The image of passion as something that gets *under the skin* — that inhabits the body from the inside. Not in the head (intellectual) or in the heart (sentimental) but in the skin, the outermost and most sensitive organ. Desire as a physical presence.
*Theme:* Love / Passion

---

**34. Jeter son bonnet par-dessus les moulins**
*Literal:* To throw one's bonnet over the windmills
*Meaning:* To throw caution to the wind; to do exactly what one pleases without caring about convention
*Example:* *"À cinquante ans, elle a tout jeté par-dessus les moulins et ouvert une boulangerie en Provence."*
*Origin:* 18th century. A young woman who threw her bonnet (the marker of respectability and modesty) over the windmills was abandoning all concern for reputation. The windmills as the mechanism of social judgment — and the bonnet flying over them as liberation.
*Theme:* Love / Freedom / Courage

---

**35. Prendre ses jambes à son cou**
*Literal:* To take one's legs to one's neck
*Meaning:* To run away as fast as possible; to flee
*Example:* *"Quand il a vu l'expression de son patron, il a pris ses jambes à son cou."*
*Origin:* The image of legs brought up to the neck — the posture of extreme speed, the runner whose legs move so fast they seem to reach their own neck. The impossible physical metaphor of maximum flight.
*Theme:* Love / Fear

---

**36. Se donner en spectacle**
*Literal:* To give oneself as a spectacle
*Meaning:* To make a scene; to embarrass oneself publicly
*Example:* *"Il s'est donné en spectacle devant tout le bureau en criant après sa secrétaire."*
*Origin:* Theatre imagery — to offer yourself as entertainment for the crowd, involuntarily. The performer who becomes the performance through loss of control.
*Theme:* Social Relations / Emotion

---

## DEATH (La Mort) — 8 expressions

---

**37. Manger les pissenlits par la racine**
*Literal:* To eat dandelions by the root
*Meaning:* To be dead and buried (to be eating dandelions from below, as a corpse)
*Example:* *"À son âge, il sera bientôt en train de manger les pissenlits par la racine."* (dark humor)
*Origin:* The dandelion (*pissenlit* — "piss-in-bed" — for its diuretic properties) grows in cemeteries as readily as anywhere. The corpse, buried below, is imagined eating the roots from underneath. Dark French humor applied to mortality.
*Theme:* Death / Dark Humor

---

**38. Passer l'arme à gauche**
*Literal:* To pass the weapon to the left
*Meaning:* To die
*Example:* *"Son grand-père a passé l'arme à gauche en mai dernier."*
*Origin:* Military. The rifle is held on the right shoulder when at attention; passing it to the left — the funereal side in military tradition (the heart side) — indicates death. The soldier's final transition.
*Theme:* Death

---

**39. Avoir un pied dans la tombe**
*Literal:* To have one foot in the grave
*Meaning:* To be very old or very ill; to be near death
*Example:* *"Il a beau avoir un pied dans la tombe, il refuse de ralentir."*
*Origin:* Universal image, but deeply rooted in French Catholic funerary culture. The grave (*la tombe*) as the threshold — one foot inside, one outside. The liminal state of the dying.
*Theme:* Death / Age

---

**40. Couper l'herbe sous le pied**
*Literal:* To cut the grass under someone's foot
*Meaning:* To cut the ground from under someone; to preempt someone's plans
*Example:* *"Elle m'a coupé l'herbe sous le pied en annonçant le projet avant moi."*
*Origin:* To cut the grass beneath a standing person's foot is to destabilize them — to remove the ground they stand on. The agricultural image of a stable foundation suddenly gone.
*Theme:* Competition / Defeat

---

**41. Enterrer la hache de guerre**
*Literal:* To bury the war axe
*Meaning:* To bury the hatchet; to make peace
*Example:* *"Ils se haïssaient depuis dix ans, mais ils ont fini par enterrer la hache de guerre."*
*Origin:* Borrowed from Native American diplomatic tradition (via French Canadian intermediaries). French fur traders learned from Iroquois and Algonquin ceremonial practices. The burying of weapons as peace ritual.
*Theme:* Death / Peace / Reconciliation

---

**42. N'avoir plus que ses yeux pour pleurer**
*Literal:* To have nothing left but one's eyes for crying
*Meaning:* To have lost everything; to be ruined; to have nothing left
*Example:* *"Après la faillite, il n't'a plus que ses yeux pour pleurer."*
*Origin:* The image of total destitution — when you have nothing, the only thing left is the capacity for grief. The eyes that weep as the final possession. French acceptance of tragedy without sentimentality.
*Theme:* Death / Loss / Ruin

---

**43. Rendre l'âme**
*Literal:* To give back the soul
*Meaning:* To die; also (humorous) for a machine to break down irreparably
*Example:* *"Ma vieille voiture a rendu l'âme sur l'autoroute."*
*Origin:* Christian theology: the soul is on loan from God; death is returning it. The idea of the soul as something that belongs ultimately to its Maker and is surrendered at death.
*Theme:* Death / Theology

---

**44. Il faut rendre à César ce qui appartient à César**
*Literal:* One must render to Caesar what belongs to Caesar
*Meaning:* Give credit where credit is due; acknowledge what someone has done or deserves
*Example:* *"Je ne l'aime pas, mais il faut rendre à César ce qui appartient à César — son travail était excellent."*
*Origin:* Matthew 22:21 — the response of Jesus to the question about Roman taxation: "Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's; and unto God the things that are God's." One of the most-quoted Biblical passages in French secular culture.
*Theme:* Justice / Fairness

---

## WIT (L'Esprit) — 14 expressions

---

**45. Revenons à nos moutons**
*Literal:* Let us return to our sheep
*Meaning:* Let's get back to the subject; let's return to the matter at hand
*Example:* *"On a beaucoup parlé d'autre chose, mais revenons à nos moutons — la réunion."*
*Origin:* From the medieval French farce *La Farce de Maître Pathelin* (c.1460), one of the most performed plays in French theatrical history. A scene in which a judge, attempting to conduct a trial about stolen sheep, keeps being interrupted by an irrelevant dispute about cloth. The judge repeatedly calls for order: *"Mais, revenons à nos moutons!"* The expression became proverbial immediately. It is six centuries old and still in daily use.
*Theme:* Wit / Focus

---

**46. Avoir la tête dans les nuages**
*Literal:* To have one's head in the clouds
*Meaning:* To be a dreamer; to be distracted; to not pay attention to reality
*Example:* *"Quand on lui parle, il a toujours la tête dans les nuages."*
*Origin:* The clouds as the opposite of the ground — the domain of the impractical, the visionary, the uncommitted to the real. Aristophanes used this image for philosophers in *The Clouds* (423 BC). The French inherited it via Latin.
*Theme:* Wit / Character

---

**47. Avoir le cafard (encore)**
Already listed under Food. Returned here for context: the cafard crosses every French thematic category.

---

**48. Courir deux lièvres à la fois**
*Literal:* To chase two hares at once
*Meaning:* To try to do two things at once and probably fail at both; to juggle incompatible goals
*Example:* *"Il essaie de courir deux lièvres à la fois — terminer sa thèse et lancer une entreprise."*
*Origin:* Hunting proverb. A hound that chases two hares simultaneously catches neither. The practical wisdom of the hunter applied to human ambition.
*Theme:* Pragmatism / Wit

---

**49. Avoir les yeux plus grands que le ventre**
*Literal:* To have eyes bigger than one's stomach
*Meaning:* To take more than one can deal with; to overestimate one's capacity
*Example:* *"J'ai pris trois desserts — j'avais les yeux plus grands que le ventre."*
*Origin:* The dining table is France's primary source of philosophical wisdom. The eyes (desire) are always larger than the stomach (capacity). This could be applied to food, business plans, romantic ambitions, or political promises.
*Theme:* Wit / Food

---

**50. C'est bonnet blanc et blanc bonnet**
*Literal:* It's white bonnet and bonnet white
*Meaning:* It's six of one and half a dozen of the other; there's no real difference
*Example:* *"Les deux candidats — c'est bonnet blanc et blanc bonnet."*
*Origin:* The chiasme as content: the phrase is its own demonstration — the two terms swapped around prove identical. The form enacts the meaning.
*Theme:* Wit / Equivalence

---

**51. Ne pas y aller avec le dos de la cuillère**
*Literal:* Not to go there with the back of the spoon
*Meaning:* To not hold back; to do something with great energy or excess
*Example:* *"Il n'y est pas allé avec le dos de la cuillère dans ses critiques."*
*Origin:* The back of a spoon is gentler, less efficient — tapping rather than scooping. To go somewhere with the *back* of the spoon would be to hold back. Not to do so = to go with full force.
*Theme:* Wit / Directness

---

**52. Vouloir le beurre, l'argent du beurre, et le sourire de la crémière**
*Literal:* To want the butter, the butter money, and the smile of the dairy woman
*Meaning:* Extended version of *avoir le beurre et l'argent du beurre* — to want everything, including the charm of the person you're taking it from
*Example:* *"Il voulait une augmentation, de meilleures conditions, et garder ses collègues sympas — vouloir le beurre, l'argent du beurre, et le sourire de la crémière."*
*Origin:* The extension of the older expression — adding the *sourire de la crémière* as the final, human demand. Not just the material goods but the goodwill of the person providing them.
*Theme:* Wit / Greed

---

**53. Donner sa langue au chat**
*Literal:* To give one's tongue to the cat
*Meaning:* To give up guessing; to admit you don't know the answer
*Example:* *"Je donne ma langue au chat — je ne sais vraiment pas qui a dit ça."*
*Origin:* In guessing games, offering your tongue to the cat was the traditional formula for giving up — surrendering the organ of speech and guessing to the cat, who would presumably dispose of it. The cat as the recipient of the confessed ignorance.
*Theme:* Wit / Games

---

**54. Il ne faut pas pousser mémé dans les orties**
*Literal:* You mustn't push granny into the nettles
*Meaning:* Don't go too far; don't take advantage; that's enough
*Example:* *"Il a accepté un délai, mais il ne faut pas pousser mémé dans les orties — il attend les résultats ce soir."*
*Origin:* The image is perfectly French: a grandmother, nettle patches, and the injunction not to push too far. The granny as the emblem of what must not be mistreated, the nettles as the consequence of going too far.
*Theme:* Wit / Limits

---

**55. Avoir le vent en poupe**
*Literal:* To have the wind in one's stern
*Meaning:* To have the wind behind you; to be going strong; to have success on your side
*Example:* *"Depuis la sortie de son livre, il a le vent en poupe."*
*Origin:* Navigation. The *poupe* (stern) is the rear of a ship. Wind in the stern means the wind is pushing you forward — the favored wind. The sailor's dream of effortless progress.
*Theme:* Wit / Success

---

**56. Qui vivra verra**
*Literal:* Whoever lives will see
*Meaning:* Time will tell; we'll see what happens
*Example:* *"Je ne sais pas comment ça va finir — qui vivra verra."*
*Origin:* Medieval proverb. The resigned patience of the person who cannot control the future — only wait and witness it. The French acceptance of uncertainty without anxiety.
*Theme:* Wit / Patience / Philosophy

---

**57. Il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours avant de l'avoir tué**
*Literal:* You mustn't sell the bear's skin before you've killed him
*Meaning:* Don't count your chickens before they hatch
*Example:* *"Il parle déjà de ses bénéfices futurs — il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours avant de l'avoir tué."*
*Origin:* La Fontaine, *L'Ours et les deux compagnons* (The Bear and the Two Companions, 1678). Two men sell a bearskin before catching the bear. The bear arrives; one man climbs a tree, the other plays dead. The bear sniffs the man and leaves (bears don't eat corpses). Moral: don't count profits not yet made.
*Theme:* Wit / Caution / La Fontaine

---

**58. Qui ne risque rien n'a rien**
*Literal:* Who risks nothing has nothing
*Meaning:* Nothing ventured, nothing gained
*Example:* *"Je ne suis pas sûr d'être prêt, mais qui ne risque rien n'a rien — je vais postuler."*
*Origin:* Proverbial. One of France's most quoted axioms — paradoxically, given French culture's preference for *la sécurité*. The French acknowledge the necessity of risk even when they personally prefer to avoid it.
*Theme:* Courage / Wit

## PHILOSOPHY (La Pensée) — 10 expressions

---

**59. L'habit ne fait pas le moine**
*Literal:* The robe doesn't make the monk
*Meaning:* Don't judge by appearances; clothes don't make the man
*Example:* *"Elle s'habille simplement, mais l'habit ne fait pas le moine — c'est la directrice financière."*
*Origin:* Medieval monastic. A man in monk's robes is not necessarily a monk — and the real monk may be recognizable by his life, not his garment.
*Theme:* Philosophy / Appearances

---

**60. À chaque jour suffit sa peine**
*Literal:* To each day is sufficient its own trouble
*Meaning:* Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof; deal with today's problems today; don't borrow trouble from tomorrow
*Example:* *"Pourquoi t'inquiéter de ce qui pourrait arriver dans six mois ? À chaque jour suffit sa peine."*
*Origin:* Matthew 6:34. One of the most-cited Biblical phrases in French secular culture — widely used by people who would not describe themselves as Christian.
*Theme:* Philosophy / Anxiety

---

**61. Tel est pris qui croyait prendre**
*Literal:* Such is caught who believed he was catching
*Meaning:* The hunter becomes the hunted; the trapper is trapped
*Example:* *"Il pensait me manipuler, mais tel est pris qui croyait prendre — je le savais depuis le début."*
*Origin:* La Fontaine, *Le Rat et l'Huître* (The Rat and the Oyster, 1679). A rat, curious about everything, investigates an open oyster shell. The shell closes on his head. Moral: curiosity without judgment leads to one's own capture.
*Theme:* Philosophy / Wit / Reversal

---

**62. On n'est jamais si bien servi que par soi-même**
*Literal:* One is never so well served as by oneself
*Meaning:* If you want something done right, do it yourself
*Example:* *"J'aurais dû m'en occuper directement — on n'est jamais si bien servi que par soi-même."*
*Origin:* Proverbial. The French individualism that underlies the apparent sociability — the conviction that ultimately, only you can guarantee what you need.
*Theme:* Philosophy / Self-Reliance

---

**63. Tout vient à point à qui sait attendre**
*Literal:* Everything comes at the right time to those who know how to wait
*Meaning:* Everything comes to those who wait
*Example:* *"Ça a pris du temps, mais elle a eu sa promotion — tout vient à point à qui sait attendre."*
*Origin:* Medieval. *À point* (at the right moment, perfectly — as in *steak à point*, medium-rare, exactly right). Patience as the wisdom of timing.
*Theme:* Philosophy / Patience

---

**64. La nuit porte conseil**
*Literal:* The night brings advice
*Meaning:* Sleep on it; things are clearer in the morning
*Example:* *"Ne prends pas ta décision ce soir — la nuit porte conseil."*
*Origin:* Ancient. The night as the unconscious counselor — sleep as the mechanism through which the mind works without interference. The wisdom that comes from rest.
*Theme:* Philosophy / Wisdom

---

**65. Les absents ont toujours tort**
*Literal:* The absent are always wrong
*Meaning:* Those who aren't present to defend themselves always get blamed; you have to be there
*Example:* *"On parle de lui sans qu'il soit là pour répondre — les absents ont toujours tort."*
*Origin:* Philippe Néricault Destouches, *L'Obstacle imprévu* (1717). A social observation about the dynamics of social judgment: the person not in the room cannot defend themselves, so their absence is attributed to guilt.
*Theme:* Philosophy / Social Life

---

**66. Nul n'est prophète en son pays**
*Literal:* No one is a prophet in their own country
*Meaning:* You are rarely recognized at home; familiarity breeds underestimation
*Example:* *"Il est plus célèbre à l'étranger qu'en France — nul n'est prophète en son pays."*
*Origin:* Luke 4:24 — Jesus in Nazareth. Also in Montaigne. One of the founding observations of French intellectual culture: true recognition comes from outside, from distance, from those who can see you without the noise of familiarity.
*Theme:* Philosophy / Recognition

---

**67. Rien ne sert de courir, il faut partir à point**
*Literal:* It is no use running; one must set out on time
*Meaning:* It's not about speed, it's about preparation; starting well is better than rushing
*Example:* *"N'attends pas la dernière minute pour préparer ton exposé — rien ne sert de courir, il faut partir à point."*
*Origin:* La Fontaine, *Le Lièvre et la Tortue* (The Hare and the Tortoise, 1668). The hare runs faster but sleeps; the tortoise plods and wins. The French version's moral is different from Aesop's: not "slow and steady" but "start at the right time." Timing over pace.
*Theme:* Philosophy / La Fontaine

---

**68. L'union fait la force**
*Literal:* Union makes strength
*Meaning:* There is strength in unity; together we are stronger
*Example:* *"L'union fait la force — c'est pourquoi les syndicats ont obtenu cette réforme."*
*Origin:* The motto of Belgium (in three languages), and a cornerstone of French Republican and socialist thought. The labor union as the institutionalization of this principle.
*Theme:* Philosophy / Solidarity

## PATIENCE (Le Temps) — 8 expressions

---

**69. Petit à petit, l'oiseau fait son nid**
*Literal:* Little by little, the bird builds its nest
*Meaning:* Step by step; gradually; patience and persistence
*Example:* *"Je ne suis pas encore fluent, mais petit à petit, l'oiseau fait son nid."*
*Origin:* La Fontaine's tradition. The bird's nest as the cumulative achievement — built one twig at a time, over days, through repetition. The language learner's motto.
*Theme:* Patience

---

**70. Paris ne s'est pas fait en un jour**
*Literal:* Paris wasn't built in a day
*Meaning:* The French version of "Rome wasn't built in a day"
*Example:* *"Tu t'attendais à être bilingue en six mois ? Paris ne s'est pas fait en un jour."*
*Origin:* The French substitution of Paris for Rome is itself revealing: for French culture, Paris is the city, the measure of civilization. The expression was known in French from the 12th century.
*Theme:* Patience / Ambition

---

**71. Il ne faut pas mettre la charrue avant les bœufs**
*Literal:* You mustn't put the cart before the oxen
*Meaning:* Don't put the cart before the horse; do things in the right order
*Example:* *"On ne peut pas ouvrir le restaurant avant d'avoir trouvé les fournisseurs — il ne faut pas mettre la charrue avant les bœufs."*
*Origin:* Agricultural France. The *charrue* (plow) is pulled by *bœufs* (oxen). To place it in front is to make it impossible to move. The proper order of things.
*Theme:* Patience / Order

---

**72. Après la pluie, le beau temps**
*Literal:* After the rain, fair weather
*Meaning:* After hardship comes better days; every cloud has a silver lining
*Example:* *"C'est difficile maintenant, mais après la pluie, le beau temps."*
*Origin:* Proverbial, ancient. The French weather system's most encouraging observation: nothing lasts. Not even the rain.
*Theme:* Patience / Hope

---

**73. Les chiens aboient, la caravane passe**
*Literal:* The dogs bark, the caravan moves on
*Meaning:* Critics can say what they want; those who are moving forward don't stop
*Example:* *"Il a eu de mauvaises critiques pour son premier roman, mais les chiens aboient, la caravane passe."*
*Origin:* Arabic proverb that entered French through North African and Ottoman trade routes. The camel caravan that crosses the desert pays no attention to the village dogs who bark at its passing.
*Theme:* Patience / Resilience

---

**74. Il faut battre le fer quand il est chaud**
*Literal:* You must strike the iron while it's hot
*Meaning:* Strike while the iron is hot; seize the moment
*Example:* *"Il est dans de bonnes dispositions ce soir — il faut battre le fer quand il est chaud."*
*Origin:* The blacksmith's technique: heated iron is malleable; cold iron resists. The moment of opportunity is the moment to act.
*Theme:* Patience / Action

---

**75. C'est en forgeant qu'on devient forgeron**
*Literal:* It's by forging that one becomes a blacksmith
*Meaning:* Practice makes perfect; you learn by doing
*Example:* *"Ne t'inquiète pas des erreurs — c'est en forgeant qu'on devient forgeron."*
*Origin:* Proverbial. The blacksmith's craft acquired by repetition — not by reading about blacksmithing but by standing at the forge. The French rejection of purely theoretical knowledge.
*Theme:* Patience / Practice / Craft

---

**76. Chaque chose en son temps**
*Literal:* Each thing in its time
*Meaning:* Everything in its proper time; one thing at a time
*Example:* *"Ne me parle pas du dessert avant qu'on ait commandé l'entrée — chaque chose en son temps."*
*Origin:* Biblical and proverbial. The temporal order as an ethical principle. The famous *"Il y a un temps pour tout"* (there is a time for everything) from Ecclesiastes is the same thought.
*Theme:* Patience / Order

## NATURE (La Campagne) — 4 expressions

---

**77. Ménager la chèvre et le chou**
*Literal:* To take care of both the goat and the cabbage
*Meaning:* To try to please everyone; to avoid taking sides; to try to reconcile incompatible things
*Example:* *"Il essaie de ménager la chèvre et le chou entre les deux factions — à terme, il mécontentera tout le monde."*
*Origin:* A river-crossing puzzle: a man must cross a river with a goat, a cabbage, and a wolf. The boat holds only one at a time. The goat eats the cabbage if left alone with it; the wolf eats the goat. How does he cross all three? The answer requires careful sequencing. The idiom takes the image of managing incompatible things — the goat and the cabbage cannot be together.
*Theme:* Nature / Diplomacy

---

**78. Quand le chat n'est pas là, les souris dansent**
*Literal:* When the cat's not there, the mice dance
*Meaning:* When the cat's away, the mice will play
*Example:* *"Le patron est en vacances — quand le chat n'est pas là, les souris dansent."*
*Origin:* Universal, ancient. The power dynamic between predator and prey, applied to hierarchy. The mice (subordinates) who change behavior in the absence of the cat (authority).
*Theme:* Nature / Hierarchy

---

**79. Il ne faut pas réveiller le chat qui dort**
*Literal:* You mustn't wake the sleeping cat
*Meaning:* Don't let sleeping dogs lie; don't stir things up unnecessarily
*Example:* *"Ne rouvre pas ce débat — il ne faut pas réveiller le chat qui dort."*
*Origin:* The French version uses a cat rather than a dog — the cat being more dangerous when suddenly awakened, more unpredictable. The sleeping cat as the dormant conflict.
*Theme:* Nature / Caution

---

**80. Il faut hurler avec les loups**
*Literal:* You must howl with the wolves
*Meaning:* When in Rome, do as the Romans do; adapt to your surroundings
*Example:* *"Je ne bois pas normalement beaucoup, mais dans ce groupe il faut hurler avec les loups."*
*Origin:* The wolves as the emblem of group behavior, collective force. To survive among wolves, you must behave as a wolf. The French pragmatic realism about social adaptation.
*Theme:* Nature / Adaptation
